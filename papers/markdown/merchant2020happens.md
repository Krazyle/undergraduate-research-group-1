<div id="abstract1" class="ltx_abstract">

###### Abstract

While there has been much recent work studying how linguistic
information is encoded in pre-trained sentence representations,
comparatively little is understood about how these models change when
adapted to solve downstream tasks. Using a suite of analysis techniques
(probing classifiers, Representational Similarity Analysis, and model
ablations), we investigate how fine-tuning affects the representations
of the BERT model. We find that while fine-tuning necessarily makes
significant changes, it does not lead to catastrophic forgetting of
linguistic phenomena. We instead find that fine-tuning primarily affects
the top layers of BERT, but with noteworthy variation across tasks. In
particular, dependency parsing reconfigures most of the model, whereas
SQuAD and MNLI appear to involve much shallower processing. Finally, we
also find that fine-tuning has a weaker effect on representations of
out-of-domain sentences, suggesting room for improvement in model
generalization.

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">1 </span>Introduction

<div id="S1.p1" class="ltx_para">

The introduction of unsupervised pre-training for Transformer
architectures
<a href="#bib.bib53" class="ltx_ref">Vaswani et al. (2017)</a> has led
to significant advances in performance on a range of NLP tasks. Most
notably, the popular BERT model
<a href="#bib.bib14" class="ltx_ref">Devlin et al. (2019)</a> topped the
GLUE <a href="#bib.bib58" class="ltx_ref">Wang et al. (2019)</a>
leaderboard when it was released, and similar models have continued to
improve scores over the past year
<a href="#bib.bib34" class="ltx_ref">Lan et al. (2019)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib44" class="ltx_ref">Raffel et al. (2019)</a>.

</div>

<div id="S1.p2" class="ltx_para">

Much recent work has attempted to better understand these models and
explain what makes them so powerful. Particularly, behavioral studies
(<a href="#bib.bib19" class="ltx_ref">Goldberg, 2019</a>,
<span id="S1.p2.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">inter
alia</span>), diagnostic probing classifiers
(<a href="#bib.bib35" class="ltx_ref">Liu et al., 2019</a>,
<span id="S1.p2.1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">inter
alia</span>), and unsupervised techniques
(<a href="#bib.bib55" class="ltx_ref">Voita et al., 2019a</a>,
<span id="S1.p2.1.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">inter alia</span>)
have shed light on the representations from the pre-trained models and
have shown that they encode a wide variety of linguistic phenomena
<a href="#bib.bib52" class="ltx_ref">Tenney et al. (2019b)</a>.

</div>

<div id="S1.p3" class="ltx_para">

However, in the standard recipe for models such as BERT
<a href="#bib.bib14" class="ltx_ref">Devlin et al. (2019)</a>, after a
Transformer is initialized with pre-trained weights, it is then trained
for a few epochs on a supervised dataset. Considerably less is
understood about what happens during this fine-tuning stage. Current
understanding is mostly derived from observations about model behavior:
fine-tuned Transformers achieve state-of-the-art performance but also
can end up learning shallow shortcuts, heuristics, and biases
<a href="#bib.bib38" class="ltx_ref">McCoy et al. (2019b)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib37" class="ltx_ref">McCoy et al. (2019a)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib21" class="ltx_ref">Gururangan et al. (2018)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib43" class="ltx_ref">Poliak et al. (2018)</a>. Thus, in
this work, we seek to understand how the internals of the model–the
representation space–change when fine-tuned for downstream tasks. We
focus on three widely-used NLP tasks: dependency parsing, natural
language inference (MNLI), and reading comprehension (SQuAD), and ask:

- <span id="S1.I1.i1"><span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">•</span></span>
  <div id="S1.I1.i1.p1" class="ltx_para">

  What happens to the encoding of linguistic features such as syntactic
  and semantic roles? Are these preserved, reinforced, or forgotten as
  the encoder learns a new task? (Section <a href="#S4" class="ltx_ref"
  title="4 What happens to linguistic features? ‣ What Happens To BERT Embeddings During Fine-tuning?"><span
  class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">4</span></a>)

  </div>
- <span id="S1.I1.i2"><span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">•</span></span>
  <div id="S1.I1.i2.p1" class="ltx_para">

  Where in the model are changes made? Are parameter updates
  concentrated in a small number of layers or are there changes
  throughout? (Section <a href="#S5" class="ltx_ref"
  title="5 What changes in the representations? ‣ What Happens To BERT Embeddings During Fine-tuning?"><span
  class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">5</span></a>)

  </div>
- <span id="S1.I1.i3"><span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">•</span></span>
  <div id="S1.I1.i3.p1" class="ltx_para">

  Do these changes generalize or does the newfound behavior only apply
  to the specific task domain? (Section <a href="#S6" class="ltx_ref"
  title="6 Out-of-Domain Behavior ‣ What Happens To BERT Embeddings During Fine-tuning?"><span
  class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">6</span></a>)

  </div>

We approach these questions with three distinct analysis techniques.
Supervised probing classifiers
(<a href="#bib.bib52" class="ltx_ref">Tenney et al., 2019b</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib25" class="ltx_ref">Hewitt and Manning, 2019</a>) give
a positive test for specific linguistic phenomena, while
Representational Similarity Analysis
(<a href="#bib.bib32" class="ltx_ref">Kriegeskorte et al., 2008</a>,
RSA; ) gives a task-agnostic measurement of the change in model
activations. Finally, we corroborate the probing and RSA results with
two types of model ablations–truncation and partial freezing–and measure
their effect on end-task performance.

</div>

<div id="S1.p4" class="ltx_para">

Taken together, we draw the following conclusions. First, linguistic
features are not lost during fine-tuning. Second, fine-tuning tends to
affect only the top few layers of BERT, albeit with significant
variation across tasks: SQuAD and MNLI have a relatively shallow effect,
while dependency parsing involves deeper changes to the encoder. We
confirm this by partial-freezing experiments which test how many layers
<span id="S1.p4.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">need</span> to
change to do well on each task and relate this to an estimate of task
<span id="S1.p4.1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">difficulty</span>
with layer ablations. Finally, we observe that fine-tuning induces large
changes on in-domain examples, but the representations of out-of-domain
sentences resemble those of the pre-trained encoder.

</div>

</div>

<div id="S2" class="section ltx_section">

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">2 </span>Related Work

<div id="S2.SS0.SSS0.Px1" class="section ltx_paragraph">

#### Base model

<div id="S2.SS0.SSS0.Px1.p1" class="ltx_para">

Many recent papers have focused on understanding sentence encoders such
as ELMo <a href="#bib.bib40" class="ltx_ref">Peters et al. (2018a)</a>
and BERT <a href="#bib.bib14" class="ltx_ref">Devlin et al. (2019)</a>,
focusing primarily on the “innate” abilities of the pre-trained (“Base”)
models. For language models that report perplexity scores, behavioral
analyses <a href="#bib.bib19" class="ltx_ref">Goldberg (2019)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib36" class="ltx_ref">Marvin and Linzen (2018)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib20" class="ltx_ref">Gulordava et al. (2018)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib16" class="ltx_ref">Ettinger (2020)</a> have shown that
they capture phenomena like number agreement and anaphora. For
Transformer-based models
<a href="#bib.bib53" class="ltx_ref">Vaswani et al. (2017)</a>, analyses
of attention weights have shown interpretable patterns in their
structure <a href="#bib.bib11" class="ltx_ref">Coenen et al. (2019)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib54" class="ltx_ref">Vig and Belinkov (2019)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib56" class="ltx_ref">Voita et al. (2019b)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib26" class="ltx_ref">Hoover et al. (2019)</a> and found
strong correlations to syntax
<a href="#bib.bib10" class="ltx_ref">Clark et al. (2019)</a>. However,
other studies have also cast doubt on what conclusions can be drawn from
attention patterns
<a href="#bib.bib27" class="ltx_ref">Jain and Wallace (2019)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib48" class="ltx_ref">Serrano and Smith (2019)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib8" class="ltx_ref">Brunner et al. (2019)</a>.

</div>

<div id="S2.SS0.SSS0.Px1.p2" class="ltx_para">

More generally, supervised probing models (also known as diagnostic
classifiers) make few assumptions beyond the existence of model
activations and can test for the presence of a wide variety of
phenomena. <a href="#bib.bib4" class="ltx_ref">Adi et al. (2016)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib7" class="ltx_ref">Blevins et al. (2018)</a> tested
recurrent networks for sentence length, word context, and syntactic
properties, while
<a href="#bib.bib12" class="ltx_ref">Conneau et al. (2018)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib28" class="ltx_ref">Jawahar et al. (2019)</a> use
another 10 probing tasks, including sensitivity to bigram shift.
<a href="#bib.bib52" class="ltx_ref">Tenney et al. (2019b)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib35" class="ltx_ref">Liu et al. (2019)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib41" class="ltx_ref">Peters et al. (2018b)</a>
introduced task suites that probe for high-level linguistic phenomena
such as part-of-speech, entity types, and coreference, while
<a href="#bib.bib51" class="ltx_ref">Tenney et al. (2019a)</a> showed
that these phenomena are represented in a hierarchical order in the
different layers of BERT.
<a href="#bib.bib25" class="ltx_ref">Hewitt and Manning (2019)</a> also
used a geometrically-motivated probing model to explore syntactic
structures, and
<a href="#bib.bib57" class="ltx_ref">Wallace et al. (2019)</a> explored
the ability of ELMo and BERT to faithfully encode numerical
information.<span id="footnote1"
class="ltx_note ltx_role_footnote"><sup>1</sup><span class="ltx_note_outer"><span class="ltx_note_content"><sup>1</sup>
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_note">1</span> See
<a href="#bib.bib6" class="ltx_ref">Belinkov and Glass (2019)</a> and
<a href="#bib.bib46" class="ltx_ref">Rogers et al. (2020)</a> for a
survey of probing methods.</span></span></span>

</div>

<div id="S2.SS0.SSS0.Px1.p3" class="ltx_para">

While probing models depend on labelled data, parallel work has studied
the same encoders using unsupervised techniques.
<a href="#bib.bib55" class="ltx_ref">Voita et al. (2019a)</a> used a
form of canonical correlation analysis
(<a href="#bib.bib39" class="ltx_ref">Morcos et al., 2018</a>, PW-CCA; )
to study the layer-wise evolution of representations, while
<a href="#bib.bib47" class="ltx_ref">Saphra and Lopez (2019)</a>
explored how these representations evolve during training.
<a href="#bib.bib3" class="ltx_ref">Abnar et al. (2019)</a> used
Representational Similarity Analysis
(<a href="#bib.bib33" class="ltx_ref">Laakso and Cottrell, 2000</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib32" class="ltx_ref">Kriegeskorte et al., 2008</a>, RSA;
) to study the effect of context on encoder representations, while
<a href="#bib.bib9" class="ltx_ref">Chrupała and Alishahi (2019)</a>
correlated them with syntax.
<a href="#bib.bib2" class="ltx_ref">Abdou et al. (2019)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib18" class="ltx_ref">Gauthier and Levy (2019)</a> also
compared these representations to fMRI and eye-tracking data.

</div>

</div>

<div id="S2.SS0.SSS0.Px2" class="section ltx_paragraph">

#### Fine-tuning

<div id="S2.SS0.SSS0.Px2.p1" class="ltx_para">

In contrast to the pre-trained models, there have been comparatively few
studies on understanding the fine-tuning process. Initial studies of
fine-tuned encoders have shown state-of-the-art performance on benchmark
suites such as GLUE
<a href="#bib.bib58" class="ltx_ref">Wang et al. (2019)</a> and
surprising sample efficiency. However, behavioral studies with challenge
sets <a href="#bib.bib38" class="ltx_ref">McCoy et al. (2019b)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib43" class="ltx_ref">Poliak et al. (2018)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib17" class="ltx_ref">Ettinger et al. (2018)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib30" class="ltx_ref">Kim et al. (2018)</a> have shown
limited ability to generalize to out-of-domain data and across syntactic
perturbations.

</div>

<div id="S2.SS0.SSS0.Px2.p2" class="ltx_para">

Looking more directly at representations,
<a href="#bib.bib5" class="ltx_ref">van Aken et al. (2019)</a> focused
on question-answering models with task-specific probing models and
clustering analysis. They found evidence of different stages of
processing in a fine-tuned BERT model but showed only limited
comparisons with the pre-trained encoder.
<a href="#bib.bib22" class="ltx_ref">Hao et al. (2019)</a> explored
fine-tuning from an optimization perspective, finding that pre-training
leads to more efficient and stable optimization than random
initialization.
<a href="#bib.bib42" class="ltx_ref">Peters et al. (2019)</a> also
analyzed the effects of fine-tuning with respect to the performance of
diagnostic classifiers at various layers.
<a href="#bib.bib18" class="ltx_ref">Gauthier and Levy (2019)</a> is
closest to our work: while focused on correlating representation to fMRI
data, they also studied fine-tuning using RSA and the structural probe
of <a href="#bib.bib25" class="ltx_ref">Hewitt and Manning (2019)</a>,
finding a significant divergence between the final representations of
models fine-tuned on different tasks. By comparison, we seek a more
general analysis of the internal representations of fine-tuned BERT
models and also focus on how they change compared to the pre-trained
Base model.

</div>

</div>

</div>

<div id="S3" class="section ltx_section">

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">3 </span>Experimental Setup

<div id="S3.SS0.SSS0.Px1" class="section ltx_paragraph">

#### BERT

<div id="S3.SS0.SSS0.Px1.p1" class="ltx_para">

In this paper, we focus on the effects of fine-tuning for the popular
BERT architecture
<a href="#bib.bib14" class="ltx_ref">Devlin et al. (2019)</a>. During
pre-training, the model is initialized by training on masked
language-modeling and next sentence prediction, with an unsupervised
corpus from BooksCorpus
<a href="#bib.bib64" class="ltx_ref">Zhu et al. (2015)</a> and English
Wikipedia. This model uses WordPiece tokenization
<a href="#bib.bib61" class="ltx_ref">Wu et al. (2016)</a> and prepends
input sentences with a `[CLS]` token, used for classification. We use
the original TensorFlow
(<a href="#bib.bib1" class="ltx_ref">Abadi et al., 2015</a>)
implementation of BERT<span id="footnote2"
class="ltx_note ltx_role_footnote"><sup>2</sup><span class="ltx_note_outer"><span class="ltx_note_content"><sup>2</sup>
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_note">2</span>
<a href="https://github.com/google-research/bert"
class="ltx_ref ltx_url ltx_font_typewriter">https://github.com/google-research/bert</a></span></span></span>
and focus on the 12-layer <span id="S3.SS0.SSS0.Px1.p1.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_typewriter">bert_base_uncased</span> variant.
We denote the pre-trained model as <span id="S3.SS0.SSS0.Px1.p1.1.3"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Base</span> and refer to fine-tuned
versions by the name of the task.

</div>

</div>

<div id="S3.SS0.SSS0.Px2" class="section ltx_paragraph">

#### MNLI

<div id="S3.SS0.SSS0.Px2.p1" class="ltx_para">

A common benchmark for natural language understanding, the MNLI dataset
<a href="#bib.bib60" class="ltx_ref">Williams et al. (2018)</a> contains
over 433K sentence pairs annotated with textual entailment information.
BERT is adapted to this task by feeding the `[CLS]` token in the last
layer through an additional output layer for predictions. We use the
parameters and architecture of
<a href="#bib.bib14" class="ltx_ref">Devlin et al. (2019)</a> for
fine-tuning. Across three trials, the evaluation accuracy of our BERT
Base model is $`83.3\pm 0.1`$, slightly lower but comparable to the
published score of $`84.6`$.

</div>

</div>

<div id="S3.SS0.SSS0.Px3" class="section ltx_paragraph">

#### SQuAD

<div id="S3.SS0.SSS0.Px3.p1" class="ltx_para">

The SQuADv1.1 dataset
<a href="#bib.bib45" class="ltx_ref">Rajpurkar et al. (2016)</a>
contains over 100,000 crowd-sourced question-answer pairs, created from
a set of Wikipedia articles. The answers are given by contiguous spans
from the original question, so the task is integrated into the BERT
framework with an additional output layer for predicting the start and
end tokens of the answer. We use the parameters and architecture of
<a href="#bib.bib14" class="ltx_ref">Devlin et al. (2019)</a> for
fine-tuning. Our average F1 score is $`89.2\pm 0.2`$, slightly higher
than the published $`88.5`$.

</div>

</div>

<div id="S3.SS0.SSS0.Px4" class="section ltx_paragraph">

#### Dependency Parsing

<div id="S3.SS0.SSS0.Px4.p1" class="ltx_para">

We also introduce a BERT model fine-tuned on dependency parsing (Dep).
We include this task to present a contrasting perspective from the prior
two datasets, in particular since prior research has suggested that much
of the information needed to solve dependency parsing is already present
in the pre-trained base
<a href="#bib.bib25" class="ltx_ref">Hewitt and Manning (2019)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib19" class="ltx_ref">Goldberg (2019)</a>. Our model is
trained on data from the CoNLL 2017 Shared Task
<a href="#bib.bib62" class="ltx_ref">Zeman et al. (2017)</a> and uses
the features of BERT as input to a biaffine classifier, similar to
<a href="#bib.bib15" class="ltx_ref">Dozat and Manning (2017)</a>. The
model uses a learning rate of $`3\times 10^{-5}`$ with a $`10\%`$
warm-up portion, uses an Adam optimizer
<a href="#bib.bib31" class="ltx_ref">Kingma and Ba (2014)</a>, and is
trained for 20 epochs. The Labeled Attachment Score (LAS) on the
development set for our models is $`96.3\pm 0.1`$.

</div>

</div>

</div>

<div id="S4" class="section ltx_section">

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">4 </span>What happens to linguistic features?

<div id="S4.p1" class="ltx_para">

Equipped with the models trained on these downstream tasks, we ask how
the representation of linguistic features in the fine-tuned models
compare to those in the pre-trained model? Recent studies have shown
that these robust features do not inform predictions on downstream
tasks, with models appearing to use dataset heuristics such as lexical
overlap <a href="#bib.bib38" class="ltx_ref">McCoy et al. (2019b)</a> or
word priors
(<a href="#bib.bib43" class="ltx_ref">Poliak et al., 2018</a>), but it
is an open question whether this is because these features are forgotten
entirely or simply are not always used. We approach this with supervised
probing models, using two complementary techniques: edge probes
(<a href="#bib.bib52" class="ltx_ref">Tenney et al., 2019b</a>) which
test for labeling information across several formalisms, and structural
probes
(<a href="#bib.bib25" class="ltx_ref">Hewitt and Manning, 2019</a>)
which measure the representation of syntactic structure.

</div>

#### Notation

<div id="S4.SS0.SSS0.Px1.p1" class="ltx_para">

Let $`m`$ be the hidden size of the attention layers of a Transformer.
Then, we define $`\bm{h}_{i}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}`$ to be the hidden
representation of the $`i`$-th word at the $`l`$-th attention layer.
Note that BERT uses subword tokenization, so word representations are
aggregated using mean pooling over all subword components.

</div>

</div>

<div id="S4.SS0.SSS0.Px2" class="section ltx_paragraph">

#### Edge Probing

<div id="S4.SS0.SSS0.Px2.p1" class="ltx_para">

The edge probing tasks of
<a href="#bib.bib52" class="ltx_ref">Tenney et al. (2019b)</a> aim to
measure how well a contextual encoder captures linguistic phenomena,
ranging from syntactic concepts such as part-of-speech to more semantic
abstractions including entity typing and relation classification. To
perform this task, the trained encoder is frozen, and the relevant
hidden states $`\bm{h}_{i}^{l}`$ are fed into an auxiliary shallow
neural network to predict labeling information. We use the tasks,
architecture, and procedure of
<a href="#bib.bib52" class="ltx_ref">Tenney et al. (2019b)</a>.<span id="footnote3"
class="ltx_note ltx_role_footnote"><sup>3</sup><span class="ltx_note_outer"><span class="ltx_note_content"><sup>3</sup>
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_note">3</span> The dependency labeling task
is from the English Web Treebank
<a href="#bib.bib49" class="ltx_ref">Silveira et al. (2014)</a>, SPR
corresponds to SPR1 from
<a href="#bib.bib50" class="ltx_ref">Teichert et al. (2017)</a>, and
relations is Task 8 from SemEval 2010
<a href="#bib.bib23" class="ltx_ref">Hendrickx et al. (2010)</a>. All of
the other tasks are from OntoNotes 5.0
<a href="#bib.bib59" class="ltx_ref">Weischedel et al. (2013)</a>.</span></span></span>
After training, we report the micro-averaged F1 scores on a held-out
test set.

</div>

</div>

<div id="S4.SS0.SSS0.Px3" class="section ltx_paragraph">

#### Structural Probe

<div id="S4.SS0.SSS0.Px3.p1" class="ltx_para">

The structural probes of
<a href="#bib.bib25" class="ltx_ref">Hewitt and Manning (2019)</a> also
analyze the token representations but are designed to evaluate how well
this space encodes syntactic structure. Specifically, the probe
identifies whether the squared L2 distance of the representations under
some linear transformation encodes the tree distances between words in
the dependency parse. The initial paper’s results showed that the deep
models (ELMo and BERT) discover this syntax information during
pre-training, which is not present in simple word embedding baselines.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS0.SSS0.Px3.p2" class="ltx_para">

The first structural probe predicts the tree depth (tree distance from
the root node) by:

|     |                                                                  |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |                                                                  
       ``` math                                                          
       ||\bm{h}_{i}^{l}||_{B}=(B\bm{h}_{i}^{l})^{\top}(B\bm{h}_{i}^{l})  
       ```                                                               |     |

where $`B\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times m}`$ is a learned
matrix.<span id="footnote4"
class="ltx_note ltx_role_footnote"><sup>4</sup><span class="ltx_note_outer"><span class="ltx_note_content"><sup>4</sup>
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_note">4</span> For our experiments, we use
a rank $`k=512`$ to match the projection dimension from the edge
probes.</span></span></span> The corresponding metrics are the
prediction accuracy of the root node and the Spearman correlation
between the predicted and true tree depths.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS0.SSS0.Px3.p3" class="ltx_para">

The second structural probe measures the pairwise distances for all
words in the parse tree. For any two words ($`\bm{h}_{i}^{l}`$,
$`\bm{h}_{j}^{l}`$), the distance is given by:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
||\bm{h}_{i}^{l}-\bm{h}_{j}^{l}||_{B}=(B(\bm{h}_{i}^{l}-\bm{h}_{j}^{l}))^{\top}(B(\bm{h}_{i}^{l}-\bm{h}_{j}^{l}))
``` |  |

Following
<a href="#bib.bib25" class="ltx_ref">Hewitt and Manning (2019)</a>, we
evaluate Spearman correlation between each row of the predicted and true
distance matricies, and also compute the minimum spanning tree and
compare to the true parse using the Undirected Unlabeled Attachment
Score (UUAS).

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1" class="section ltx_subsection">

### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_subsection">4.1 </span>Results

<div id="S4.SS1.p1" class="ltx_para">

The results from both probing tasks largely demonstrate that the
linguistic structures from pre-training are preserved in the fine-tuned
models. This is first seen in the edge probing metrics presented in
Table <a href="#S4.T1" class="ltx_ref"
title="Table 1 ‣ 4 What happens to linguistic features? ‣ What Happens To BERT Embeddings During Fine-tuning?"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">1</span></a>. For the sake of comparison,
we also provide baseline results on the output of the embedding layer
(Lexical) and a randomly initialized BERT architecture (Randomized).
These baselines are important because inspection-based analysis can
often discover patterns that are not obviously present due to the high
capacity of auxiliary classifiers. For example,
<a href="#bib.bib63" class="ltx_ref">Zhang and Bowman (2018)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib24" class="ltx_ref">Hewitt and Liang (2019)</a> found
that expressive-enough probing methods can have decent performance even
when trained on random noise.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1.p2" class="ltx_para">

Across the edge probing suite, for all three tasks we see only small
changes in F1 score compared to BERT base. In most cases we observe a
drop in performance of 0.5-2%, with some variation: MNLI and SQuAD lead
to drops of 1.5-3% on syntactic tasks–constituents, and POS,
dependencies, and SRL, respectively–while the dependency parsing model
leads to significantly improved syntactic performance (+4% on
constituent labeling) while dropping performance on the more
semantically-oriented coreference, SPR, and relation classification
tasks. Nonetheless, in all cases these effects are small: they are
comparable to the variation between randomly-seeded fine-tuning runs
($`\pm 0.7`$), and much smaller than the difference between the full
model and the Lexical or Randomized baselines, suggesting that most
linguistic information from BERT is still available after fine-tuning.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1.p3" class="ltx_para">

Next, we turn to the structural probe, with results seen in Figure
<a href="#S4.F2" class="ltx_ref"
title="Figure 2 ‣ Structural Probe ‣ 4 What happens to linguistic features? ‣ What Happens To BERT Embeddings During Fine-tuning?"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">2</span></a>. First, the dependency parsing
fine-tuned model shows improvements in both the correlation and the
absolute metrics of root accuracy and UUAS, as early as layer 5. Since
the structural probes are designed and trained to look for syntax, this
result suggests that the fine-tuning improves the model’s internal
representation of such information. This makes intuitive sense as the
fine-tuning task is aligned with the probing task.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1.p4" class="ltx_para">

On the MNLI and SQuAD fine-tuned models, we observe small drops in
performance, particularly with the final layer. These changes are most
pronounced on the root accuracy and UUAS metrics, which score against a
discrete decoded solution (`argmin` for root accuracy or minimum
spanning tree for UUAS), but are smaller in magnitude on Spearman
correlations which consider all predictions. This suggests that while
some information is lost, the actual magnitude of change within the
“syntactic subspace” is quite small. This is consistent with
observations by
<a href="#bib.bib18" class="ltx_ref">Gauthier and Levy (2019)</a> and
suggests that information about syntactic structure is well-preserved in
end-task models.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1.p5" class="ltx_para">

Overall, the results from these two probing techniques suggest that
there is no catastrophic forgetting. This is surprising as a number of
prior error analyses have shown that the fine-tuned models often do not
use syntax <a href="#bib.bib38" class="ltx_ref">McCoy et al. (2019b)</a>
and rely on annotation artifacts
<a href="#bib.bib21" class="ltx_ref">Gururangan et al. (2018)</a> or
simple pattern matching
(<a href="#bib.bib29" class="ltx_ref">Jia and Liang, 2017</a>) to solve
downstream tasks. Our analysis suggests that the while this linguistic
information may not be incorporated into the final predictions, it is
still available in the model’s representations.

</div>

</div>

</div>

<div id="S5" class="section ltx_section">

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">5 </span>What changes in the representations?

<div id="S5.p1" class="ltx_para">

Supervised probes are highly targeted: as trained models, they are
sensitive to particular linguistic phenomena, but they also can learn to
ignore everything else. If the supervised probe is closely related to
the fine-tuning task–such as for syntactic probes and a dependency
parsing model–we have observed significant changes in performance, but
otherwise we see little effect. Nonetheless, we know that
<span id="S5.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">something</span>
must be changing when fine-tuning–as evidenced by prior work that shows
that end-task performance degrades if the encoder is completely frozen
<a href="#bib.bib42" class="ltx_ref">Peters et al. (2019)</a>. To
explore this change more broadly, we turn to an unsupervised technique,
Representational Similarity Analysis, and corroborate our findings with
layer-based ablations.

</div>

<div id="S5.SS1" class="section ltx_subsection">

### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_subsection">5.1 </span>Representational Similarity Analysis

<div id="S5.SS1.p1" class="ltx_para">

Representational Similarity Analysis
(<a href="#bib.bib33" class="ltx_ref">Laakso and Cottrell, 2000</a>,
RSA; ) is a technique for measuring the similarity between two different
representation spaces for a given set of stimuli. Originally developed
for neuroscience
<a href="#bib.bib32" class="ltx_ref">Kriegeskorte et al. (2008)</a>, it
has become increasingly used to analyze similarity between neural
network activations
(<a href="#bib.bib3" class="ltx_ref">Abnar et al., 2019</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib9" class="ltx_ref">Chrupała and Alishahi, 2019</a>).
The method works by using a common set of $`n`$ examples, used to create
two comparable sets of representations. Using two kernels (possibly
different for each representation space) to measure the similarity of
paired examples, the sets of representations are then converted into two
pairwise similarity matrices in $`\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}`$. The final
similarity score between the two representation spaces is calculated as
the Pearson correlation between the flattened upper triangulars of the
two similarity matrices.

</div>

<div id="S5.SS1.p2" class="ltx_para">

In our application, we pass ordinary sentences (Wikipedia),
sentence-pairs (MNLI), or question-answer pairs (SQuAD) as inputs to the
BERT model, and select a random subset ($`n=5000`$) of tokens as our
stimuli. This choice of input is consistent with the masked language
model pre-training and various fine-tuning tasks (such as SQuAD and
dependency parsing) in analyzing the contextual representations for
every token. We extract representations as the activations of
corresponding layers from the two models to compare (e.g. Base vs. a
fine-tuned model). Following previous applications of RSA to text
representations
(<a href="#bib.bib3" class="ltx_ref">Abnar et al., 2019</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib9" class="ltx_ref">Chrupała and Alishahi, 2019</a>), we
adopt cosine similarity as the kernel for all of our experiments.

</div>

<div id="S5.SS1.p3" class="ltx_para">

While RSA does not require learning any parameters and is thus resistant
to overfitting
<a href="#bib.bib2" class="ltx_ref">Abdou et al. (2019)</a>, the
unsupervised technique is sensitive to spurious signals in the
representations that may not be relevant to model
behavior.<span id="footnote5"
class="ltx_note ltx_role_footnote"><sup>5</sup><span class="ltx_note_outer"><span class="ltx_note_content"><sup>5</sup>
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_note">5</span> We note that probing
techniques are more robust to this, since they learn to focus on
relevant features.</span></span></span> To mitigate this, we repeat the
BERT pre-training procedure (as described in Section 3 of
(<a href="#bib.bib14" class="ltx_ref">Devlin et al., 2019</a>)) from
scratch three times. For each pre-trained checkpoints, we fine-tune on
the three downstream task and report the average for these independent
runs.

</div>

<img src="2004.14448v1/figures/rsa/wikipedia.png" id="S5.F3.g1"
class="ltx_graphics ltx_img_square" style="aspect-ratio:628/628;"

<div id="S5.SS1.SSS0.Px1.p1" class="ltx_para">

Figure <a href="#S5.F3" class="ltx_ref"

class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3</span></a> shows the results of our RSA
analysis comparing the three task models,
<span id="S5.SS1.SSS0.Px1.p1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Dep.</span>,
<span id="S5.SS1.SSS0.Px1.p1.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">MNLI</span>, and
<span id="S5.SS1.SSS0.Px1.p1.1.3"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">SQuAD</span>, to BERT
<span id="S5.SS1.SSS0.Px1.p1.1.4"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Base</span> at each layer, using
single-sentence inputs randomly selected from English Wikipedia. Across

Except for the output layer which is particularly sensitive to the form
of the output (span-based for dependencies and SQuAD, or using the

to the model: the second-to-last attention layer still shows a very high
similarity score of $`0.84\pm 0.02`$ compared to the representations of

yet still relatively shallow reconfiguration of the encoder.

</div>

Finally, dependency parsing presents a dissimilar pattern: we observe
the deepest changes, departing from the Base model as early as layers 4
and 5. This is consistent with our supervised probing observations

</div>

<div id="S5.SS2.p1" class="ltx_para">

As an unsupervised, metric-based technique, RSA tells us about broad
changes in the representation space, but does not in itself say if these
changes are important for the model’s behavior–i.e. for the processing
necessary to solve the downstream task. To measure our observations in
terms of task performance, we turn to two layer ablation studies.

</div>

<div id="S5.SS2.p2" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S5.SS2.p2.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Partial
Freezing</span> can be thought of as a test for how many layers
<span id="S5.SS2.p2.1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">need</span> to
change for a downstream task. We freeze the bottom $`k`$ layers (and the
embeddings)–treating them as features–but allow the rest to adapt.
Effectively, this clamps the first $`k`$ layers to have RSA similarity
of 1 with the Base model. Also, we perform <span id="S5.SS2.p2.1.3"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">model truncation</span> as a rough
estimate of difficulty for each task, and as an attempt to de-couple the
results of partial freezing from helpful features that may be available
in top layers of BERT Base
(<a href="#bib.bib51" class="ltx_ref">Tenney et al., 2019a</a>).
Figure <a href="#S5.F4" class="ltx_ref"
title="Figure 4 ‣ 5.2 Layer Ablations ‣ 5 What changes in the representations? ‣ What Happens To BERT Embeddings During Fine-tuning?"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">4</span></a> (partial freezing)
Figure <a href="#S5.F5" class="ltx_ref"
title="Figure 5 ‣ 5.2 Layer Ablations ‣ 5 What changes in the representations? ‣ What Happens To BERT Embeddings During Fine-tuning?"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">5</span></a> (truncation) show the effect
on task performance.

</div>

<div id="S5.SS2.p3" class="ltx_para">

The patterns we observe corroborate the findings of our RSA analysis. On
MNLI, we find that performance does not drop significantly unless the
last two layers are frozen, while the truncated models are able to
achieve comparable performance with only three attention layers. This
suggests that while natural language inference
<a href="#bib.bib13" class="ltx_ref">Dagan et al. (2006)</a> is known to
be a complex task <span id="S5.SS2.p3.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">in the limit</span>, most MNLI examples
can be resolved with relatively shallow processing. SQuAD exhibits a
similar trend: we see a significant performance drop when 3 or fewer
layers are allowed to change (e.g. freezing through layer 8 or higher),
consistent with where RSA finds the greatest change. From our truncation
experiment, we similarly see that only five layers are needed to achieve
comparable performance to the full model.

</div>

Dependency parsing performance drops more rapidly still–in both
experiments–again consistent with the previous results from RSA. This is
perhaps surprising, since probing analysis
<a href="#bib.bib19" class="ltx_ref">Goldberg (2019)</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib36" class="ltx_ref">Marvin and Linzen (2018)</a>

pre-trained model, and diagnostics for dependency parsing in particular
(<a href="#bib.bib52" class="ltx_ref">Tenney et al., 2019b</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib51" class="ltx_ref">Tenney et al., 2019a</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib25" class="ltx_ref">Hewitt and Manning, 2019</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib10" class="ltx_ref">Clark et al., 2019</a>) show strong

class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">2</span></a>) there is much headroom
available, and it appears that to capture it requires changing deeper
parts of the model. We hypothesize that this effect may come from the
hierarchical nature of parsing, which requires additional layers to
determine the tree-like structure, and fully reconciling these
observations would be a promising direction for future work.

</div>

<div id="S6" class="section ltx_section">

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">6 </span>Out-of-Domain Behavior

<div id="S6.p1" class="ltx_para">

Finally, we ask whether the effects of fine-tuning are general: do they
apply only to in-domain inputs, or do they lead to broader changes in
behavior? This is usually explored by behavioral methods, in which a
model is trained on one domain and evaluated on another–for example, the
mismatched evaluation for MNLI
(<a href="#bib.bib60" class="ltx_ref">Williams et al., 2018</a>)–but
this analysis is limited by the availability of labeled data. By using
Representational Similarity Analysis, we can test this in an
unsupervised manner.

</div>

<div id="S6.p2" class="ltx_para">

We use RSA to compare the fine-tuned model to Base and observe the
degree of similarity when inputs are drawn from different corpora. We
use random samples from the development sets for MNLI (as
<span id="S6.p2.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_typewriter">premise
\[SEP\] hypothesis</span>) and SQuAD (as <span id="S6.p2.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_typewriter">question \[SEP\] passage</span>) as
in-domain for their respective models,<span id="footnote6"
class="ltx_note ltx_role_footnote"><sup>6</sup><span class="ltx_note_outer"><span class="ltx_note_content"><sup>6</sup>
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_note">6</span> Note that these are unseen
during fine-tuning, although RSA scores do not change significantly if
the MNLI or SQuAD training sets are used.</span></span></span> and as
the out-of-domain control we use random Wikipedia sentences (which
resemble the pre-training domain). As in
Section <a href="#S5.SS1" class="ltx_ref"
title="5.1 Representational Similarity Analysis ‣ 5 What changes in the representations? ‣ What Happens To BERT Embeddings During Fine-tuning?"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">5.1</span></a>, we use the representations
of $`n=5000`$ tokens as our stimuli for each
comparison.<span id="footnote7"
class="ltx_note ltx_role_footnote"><sup>7</sup><span class="ltx_note_outer"><span class="ltx_note_content"><sup>7</sup>
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_note">7</span> We also tested
single-sentence examples from MNLI and SQuAD by only taking the premise
and question respectively; the trends were similar to
Figure <a href="#S5.F6" class="ltx_ref"
title="Figure 6 ‣ 5.2 Layer Ablations ‣ 5 What changes in the representations? ‣ What Happens To BERT Embeddings During Fine-tuning?"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">6</span></a>. </span></span></span> Results
for the MNLI and SQuAD fine-tuned models are shown in
Figure <a href="#S5.F6" class="ltx_ref"
title="Figure 6 ‣ 5.2 Layer Ablations ‣ 5 What changes in the representations? ‣ What Happens To BERT Embeddings During Fine-tuning?"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">6</span></a>.

</div>

<div id="S6.p3" class="ltx_para">

We note that in both cases, the trend follows
Section <a href="#S5.SS1" class="ltx_ref"
title="5.1 Representational Similarity Analysis ‣ 5 What changes in the representations? ‣ What Happens To BERT Embeddings During Fine-tuning?"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">5.1</span></a> in that the models diverge
from BERT Base in the top layers, but there is a significantly larger
change in representations on the in-domain examples. When evaluated on
Wikipedia sentences, which resemble the pre-training data, the
similarity scores are much higher. This suggests that fine-tuning leads
the model to change its representations for the fine-tuning domain but
to continue to behave more like the Base model otherwise.

</div>

</div>

<div id="S7" class="section ltx_section">

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">7 </span>Conclusions

<div id="S7.p1" class="ltx_para">

In this paper, we employ three complementary analysis methods to gain
insight on the effects of fine-tuning on the representations produced by
BERT. From supervised probing analyses, we find that the linguistic
structures discovered during pre-training remain available after
fine-tuning. Prior studies
(<a href="#bib.bib38" class="ltx_ref">McCoy et al., 2019b</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib29" class="ltx_ref">Jia and Liang, 2017</a>) have shown
that end-task models often fall back on simple heuristics; taken
together, our results suggest that while present, perhaps the linguistic
features are not always incorporated into final predictions.

</div>

<div id="S7.p2" class="ltx_para">

Next, our results using RSA and layer ablations show that the changes
from fine-tuning alter a fraction of the model capacity, specifically
within the top few layers (up to some variation across tasks). Also,
although fine-tuning has a significant affect on the representations of
in-domain sentences, the representations of out-of-domain examples
remain much closer to those of the pre-trained model.

</div>

<div id="S7.p3" class="ltx_para">

Overall, these conclusions suggest that fine-tuning–as currently
practiced–is a conservative process: largely preserving linguistic
features, affecting only a few layers, and specific to in-domain
examples. While the standard fine-tuning recipe undeniably leads to
strong performance on many tasks, there appears to be room for
improvement: an opportunity to refine this transfer step–potentially by
utilizing more of the model capacity–to better the generalization and
transferability.

</div>
