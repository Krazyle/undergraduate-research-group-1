<div id="abstract1" class="ltx_abstract">

###### Abstract

Linear scalarization, i.e., combining all loss functions by a weighted
sum, has been the default choice in the literature of multi-task
learning (MTL) since its inception. In recent years, there is a surge of
interest in developing Specialized Multi-Task Optimizers (SMTOs) that
treat MTL as a multi-objective optimization problem. However, it remains
open whether there is a fundamental advantage of SMTOs over
scalarization. In fact, heated debates exist in the community comparing
these two types of algorithms, mostly from an empirical perspective. To
approach the above question, in this paper, we revisit scalarization
from a theoretical perspective. We focus on linear MTL models and study
whether scalarization is capable of <span id="abstract1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">fully exploring</span> the Pareto
front. Our findings reveal that, in contrast to recent works that
claimed empirical advantages of scalarization, scalarization is
inherently incapable of full exploration, especially for those Pareto
optimal solutions that strike the balanced trade-offs between multiple
tasks. More concretely, when the model is under-parametrized, we reveal
a <span id="abstract1.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">multi-surface</span> structure of the
feasible region and identify necessary and sufficient conditions for
full exploration. This leads to the conclusion that scalarization is in
general incapable of tracing out the Pareto front. Our theoretical
results partially answer the open questions in Xin et al. (2021), and
provide a more intuitive explanation on why scalarization fails beyond
non-convexity. We additionally perform experiments on a real-world
dataset using both scalarization and state-of-the-art SMTOs. The
experimental results not only corroborate our theoretical findings, but
also unveil the potential of SMTOs in finding balanced solutions, which
cannot be achieved by scalarization.

</div>

<div id="S1" class="section ltx_section">

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">1 </span>Introduction

<div id="S1.p1" class="ltx_para">

When labeled data from multiple tasks are available, multi-task learning
(MTL) aims to learn a joint model for these tasks simultaneously in
order to improve
generalization (<a href="#bib.bib3" class="ltx_ref">Caruana, 1997</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib53" class="ltx_ref">Zhang and Yang, 2021</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib36" class="ltx_ref">Ruder, 2017</a>). One common
approach towards this goal is learning a joint feature representation
that is shared among multiple tasks, where each task then uses a
separate task-specific head for its own prediction. The hope is that by
sharing a joint feature representation, the effective sample size for
feature learning will be larger than that for single-task learning,
hence leading to better generalization across related tasks. This simple
and natural idea has proven to be successful in many applications,
including object
segmentation (<a href="#bib.bib30" class="ltx_ref">Misra et al., 2016</a>),
natural language
processing (<a href="#bib.bib7" class="ltx_ref">Collobert and Weston, 2008</a>),
autonomous
driving (<a href="#bib.bib48" class="ltx_ref">Yu et al., 2020a</a>), to
name a few.

</div>

<div id="S1.p2" class="ltx_para">

One typical approach to learning the shared feature model from multiple
tasks is called *linear scalarization*, where the learner provides a set
of convex coefficients (in the form of a probability vector) to linearly
combine the loss from each task and reduces the learning problem into a
scalar optimization problem. Since its inception, scalarization has been
the default approach for
MTL (<a href="#bib.bib3" class="ltx_ref">Caruana, 1997</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib36" class="ltx_ref">Ruder, 2017</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib52" class="ltx_ref">Zhang and Yang, 2018</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib8" class="ltx_ref">Crawshaw, 2020</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib53" class="ltx_ref">Zhang and Yang, 2021</a>), mainly
due to its simplicity, scalability, as well as empirical success.
However, in recent years, variants of the multiple gradient descent
algorithm
(MGDA) (<a href="#bib.bib13" class="ltx_ref">Fliege and Svaiter, 2000</a>)
have been proposed and used in
MTL (<a href="#bib.bib37" class="ltx_ref">Sener and Koltun, 2018</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib49" class="ltx_ref">Yu et al., 2020b</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib5" class="ltx_ref">Chen et al., 2020</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib23" class="ltx_ref">Liu et al., 2021a</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib24" class="ltx_ref">Liu et al., 2021b</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib55" class="ltx_ref">Zhou et al., 2022</a>). Referred to
as specialized multi-task optimizers (SMTOs), the main idea behind them
is to cast the learning problem explicitly as a multi-objective
(multi-criteria) optimization problem, wherein the goal is to enforce
convergence to a Pareto optimal solution across tasks. In the
literature, a main driving force behind these studies has been the
belief that they are better than linear scalarization when *conflicting
gradients* among tasks are
present (<a href="#bib.bib49" class="ltx_ref">Yu et al., 2020b</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib23" class="ltx_ref">Liu et al., 2021a</a>).
Nevertheless, recent empirical results tell an opposite
story (<a href="#bib.bib18" class="ltx_ref">Kurin et al., 2022</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib46" class="ltx_ref">Xin et al., 2022</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib19" class="ltx_ref">Lin et al., 2022</a>)—with proper
choices of hyperparameters and regularization techniques, scalarization
matches or even surpasses SMTOs. Hence, it largely remains an open
problem regarding whether there is an inherent advantage of using SMTOs
for MTL over scalarization, at least from a theoretical perspective.

</div>

<div id="S1.p3" class="ltx_para">

In this paper, we revisit scalarization from a theoretical perspective.
We study whether scalarization is capable of fully exploring the Pareto
front in linear MTL. Technically, we aim to understand the following
question on *full exploration*:

> <span id="S1.p3.2.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">For every
> Pareto optimum $`v`$ on the Pareto front induced by multiple tasks,
> does there exist a weighted combination of task losses such that the
> optimal solution of the linearly scalarized objective corresponds to
> $`v`$?</span>

Our fundamental thesis is that, if the answer to the above question is
affirmative, then there is no inherent advantage of SMTOs over
scalarization on the representation level; otherwise, there might be
cases where SMTOs can converge to Pareto optimal solutions that cannot
be obtained via any scalarization objective. Our findings suggest that,
in contrast to recent works that claimed empirical advantages of
scalarization, it is inherently incapable of full exploration.
Specifically, our contributions are as follows:

1.  <span id="S1.I1.i1"><span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">1.</span></span>
    <div id="S1.I1.i1.p1" class="ltx_para">

    We reveal a <span id="S1.I1.i1.p1.1.1"
    class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">multi-surface</span> structure of
    the feasible region, and identify a common failure mode of
    scalarization, which we refer to as “gradient disagreement”
    (<a href="#S3.SS1" class="ltx_ref"
    title="3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
    class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
    class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.1</span></a>). This serves as an
    important first step towards understanding when and why
    scalarization fails to achieve a specific Pareto optimum.

    </div>
2.  <span id="S1.I1.i2"><span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">2.</span></span>
    <div id="S1.I1.i2.p1" class="ltx_para">

    We provide necessary and sufficient conditions for scalarization to
    fully explore the Pareto front, which lead to the conclusion that
    scalarization is incapable of tracing out the Pareto front in
    general (<a href="#S3.SS2" class="ltx_ref"
    title="3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
    class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
    class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.2</span></a>). To the best of our
    knowledge, we are the first to establish both necessary and
    sufficient conditions for full exploration in the non-convex
    setting.

    </div>
3.  <span id="S1.I1.i3"><span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">3.</span></span>
    <div id="S1.I1.i3.p1" class="ltx_para">

    We proceed to discuss implications of our theoretical results
    (<a href="#S4.SS1" class="ltx_ref"
    title="4.1 Implications of the theoretical results ‣ 4 Discussion ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
    class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
    class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">4.1</span></a>), demonstrating how they
    answer the open questions
    in <a href="#bib.bib46" class="ltx_ref">Xin et al. (2022)</a>, and
    provide a remedy solution via randomization to counteract the
    fundamental limitation of scalarization
    (<a href="#S4.SS2" class="ltx_ref"
    title="4.2 A remedy solution ‣ 4 Discussion ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
    class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
    class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">4.2</span></a>).

    </div>
4.  <span id="S1.I1.i4"><span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">4.</span></span>
    <div id="S1.I1.i4.p1" class="ltx_para">

    We additionally perform experiments on a real-world dataset using
    both scalarization and state-of-the-art SMTOs
    (<a href="#S5" class="ltx_ref"
    title="5 Experiments ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
    class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span

    not only corroborate our theoretical findings, but also demonstrate

    </div>

<div id="S2" class="section ltx_section">

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">2 </span>Preliminaries

<div id="S2.p1" class="ltx_para">

We introduce the setup of multi-task learning (MTL) and relevant
notations, along with essential background on the optimal value of the
scalarization objective and the concept of Pareto optimality.

</div>

<div id="S2.p2" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S2.p2.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Problem
setup.</span> Following prior works, we consider multi-task learning
with $`k`$ regression
tasks (<a href="#bib.bib44" class="ltx_ref">Wu et al., 2020</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib10" class="ltx_ref">Du et al., 2021</a>). The training
examples $`\{(x_{j},y_{1}^{j},\cdots,y_{k}^{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}`$ are drawn
i.i.d. from some
$`\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{Y}_{k})`$,
where $`\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}`$ is the <span id="S2.p2.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">shared</span> input space and
$`\mathcal{Y}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}`$ is the output space for task
$`i`$. We denote the label of task $`i`$ as
$`y_{i}=(y_{i}^{1},\cdots,y_{i}^{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}`$, and
concatenate the inputs and labels in matrix forms:
$`X=[x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}`$ and
$`Y=[y_{1},\cdots,y_{k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}`$.

</div>

<div id="S2.p3" class="ltx_para">

We focus on linear MTL, in which a linear neural network serves as the
backbone for prediction. Despite its simplicity, this setting has
attracted extensive research interest in the
literature (<a href="#bib.bib28" class="ltx_ref">Maurer, 2006</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib29" class="ltx_ref">Maurer et al., 2016</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib44" class="ltx_ref">Wu et al., 2020</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib10" class="ltx_ref">Du et al., 2021</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib39" class="ltx_ref">Tripuraneni et al., 2021</a>).
Without loss of generality, the model that will be used for our study is
a two-layer linear network<span id="footnote1"
class="ltx_note ltx_role_footnote"><sup>1</sup><span class="ltx_note_outer"><span class="ltx_note_content"><sup>1</sup>
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_note">1</span> Any deep linear networks for
MTL can be reduced to the case of a two-layer linear network, with one
layer of shared representations and one layer of task-specific
heads.</span></span></span>, which consists of a shared layer
$`W\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}`$ and a task-specific head
$`a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}`$ for each task. The prediction for task $`i`$
on input $`x`$ is given by $`f_{i}(x,W,a_{i})\coloneqq x^{\top}Wa_{i}`$.
We adopt Mean Squared Error (MSE) as the loss function, and the training
loss for task $`i`$ is given by
$`L_{i}(W,a_{i})\coloneqq\|XWa_{i}-y_{i}\|_{2}^{2}`$.

</div>

<div id="S2.p4" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S2.p4.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Notation.</span> We
use $`[k]`$ to denote the index set $`\{1,\cdots,k\}`$. For two square
matrices $`M`$ and $`N`$ with the same dimension, we write
$`M\succeq N`$ if $`M-N`$ is positive semi-definite, and $`M\succ N`$ if
$`M-N`$ is positive definite. We denote $`\diag(D)`$ as the diagonal
matrix whose diagonal entries are given by $`D=\{d_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}`$, a
set of real numbers; and $`\operatorname{span}(V)`$ as the linear space
spanned by $`V=\{v_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}`$, a set of vectors. For two vectors
with the same size, we use $`\odot`$ and $`\oslash`$ to denote
entry-wise multiplication and division. When there is no ambiguity,
operations on vectors (e.g., absolute value, square, square root) are
performed entry-wise. For any matrix $`M`$, $`M^{{\dagger}}`$ stands for
its Moore-Penrose inverse. We use $`\operatorname{range}(M)`$ and
$`\operatorname{rank}(M)`$ to denote its column space and rank,
respectively. The expression $`\|\cdot\|`$ denotes the $`\ell_{2}`$ norm
of a vector, $`\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle`$ the inner product of two
vectors, $`\|\cdot\|_{F}`$ the Frobenius norm of a matrix, and
$`\rho(\cdot)`$ the spectral radius of a square matrix.

</div>

<div id="S2.p5" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S2.p5.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Optimal value of
scalarization (restated
from <a href="#bib.bib44" class="ltx_ref">Wu et al. (2020)</a>).</span> 
The objective function of scalarization is a weighted sum of losses:
$`\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}L_{i}(W,a_{i})`$. Here, the convex
coefficients $`\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}`$ satisfy $`\lambda_{i}\geq 0`$
and $`\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=1`$, and are typically obtained via grid
search (<a href="#bib.bib40" class="ltx_ref">Vandenhende et al., 2021</a>).
For each task, we can decompose its loss based on the orthogonality
principle (<a href="#bib.bib17" class="ltx_ref">Kay, 1993</a>)

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
L_{i}(W,a_{i})\coloneqq\|XWa_{i}-y_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=\|XWa_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}\|^{2}+\|\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i}\|^{2},
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(1)</span> |

where $`\hat{y}_{i}=X(X^{\top}X)^{\dagger}X^{\top}y_{i}`$ is the optimal
predictor for task $`i`$. Denote
$`\Lambda=\text{diag}(\{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{k})`$,
$`A=[a_{1},\cdots,a_{k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times k}`$,
$`\hat{Y}=[\hat{y}_{1},\cdots,\hat{y}_{k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}`$.
The scalarization objective can be compactly expressed as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\|(XWA-Y)\Lambda\|_{F}^{2}=\|(XWA-\hat{Y})\Lambda\|_{F}^{2}+\|(\hat{Y}-Y)\Lambda\|_{F}^{2}.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(2)</span> |

Throughout this paper, we assume the optimal linear predictions
$`\{\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}`$ are linearly
independent.<span id="footnote2"
class="ltx_note ltx_role_footnote"><sup>2</sup><span class="ltx_note_outer"><span class="ltx_note_content"><sup>2</sup>
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_note">2</span> As long as $`n\geq k`$, an
arbitrarily small perturbation can make $`\{\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}`$
linearly independent. We mainly adopt this assumption for ease of
presentation. </span></span></span> This leads to two distinct cases,
depending on the width of the hidden layer $`q`$:

1.  <span id="S2.I1.i1"><span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">1.</span></span>
    <div id="S2.I1.i1.p1" class="ltx_para">

    When $`q\geq k`$, the minimum value of the first term is zero, i.e.,
    every task can simultaneously achieve its optimal loss
    $`\|\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i}\|^{2}`$.

    </div>
2.  <span id="S2.I1.i2"><span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">2.</span></span>
    <div id="S2.I1.i2.p1" class="ltx_para">

    When $`q<k`$, the minimum value of the first term is given by the
    rank-$`q`$ approximation. In particular, let
    $`\hat{Y}\Lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{i}u_{i}v_{i}^{\top}`$ be the
    SVD of $`\hat{Y}\Lambda`$. According to the Eckart–Young–Mirsky
    Theorem (<a href="#bib.bib11" class="ltx_ref">Eckart and Young, 1936</a>),
    the best rank-$`q`$ approximation of $`\hat{Y}\Lambda`$ under the
    Frobenius norm is given by the truncated SVD:
    $`\hat{Y}_{q,\Lambda}\Lambda\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{q}\sigma_{i}u_{i}v_{i}^{\top}`$.
    Hence, the minimum training risk is
    $`\|(\hat{Y}_{q,\Lambda}-\hat{Y})\Lambda\|_{F}^{2}+\|(\hat{Y}-Y)\Lambda\|_{F}^{2}`$.
    This regime, referred to as the <span id="S2.I1.i2.p1.1.1"
    class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">under-parameterized</span> regime,
    constitutes the main focus of our study.

    </div>

</div>

<div id="S2.p6" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S2.p6.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Pareto
optimality.</span>  In the context of MTL, a point $`\theta^{*}`$
(shared model parameters) is a <span id="S2.p6.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">Pareto optimum</span> (PO) if there
does not exist a $`\theta^{\prime}`$ that achieves strictly better
performance on at least one task and no worse performance on all other
tasks compared to $`\theta^{*}`$. Formally, for a set of objectives
$`\{L_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}`$ that need to be minimized, if
$`L_{i}(\theta^{\prime})<L_{i}(\theta^{*})`$ for some point
$`\theta^{\prime}`$, then there must exist another task $`j`$ such that
$`L_{j}(\theta^{\prime})>L_{j}(\theta^{*})`$. The <span id="S2.p6.1.3"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">Pareto front</span> (a.k.a. Pareto
frontier) is the collection of objective values
$`(L_{1}(\theta),\cdots,L_{k}(\theta))`$ corresponding to all PO.

</div>

</div>

<div id="S3" class="section ltx_section">

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">3 </span>Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front

<div id="S3.p1" class="ltx_para">

In this section, we provide a fine-grained analysis of the feasible
region (formally defined in <a href="#S3.SS1" class="ltx_ref"
title="3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.1</span></a>), revealing its
multi-surface structure. We proceed to analyze whether scalarization is
capable of tracing out the Pareto front, and identify necessary and
sufficient conditions for full exploration.

</div>

<div id="S3.p2" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S3.p2.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Reformulation.</span>  Recall that
minimizing <a href="#S2.E1" class="ltx_ref"
title="In 2 Preliminaries ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Equation</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">1</span></a> is equivalent to minimizing
the squared loss $`\|XWa_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}\|^{2}`$, where
$`\hat{y}_{i}=X(X^{\top}X)^{\dagger}X^{\top}y_{i}`$ is the optimal
linear predictor for task $`i`$. Additionally, the task-specific heads
can be optimized independently for each task. Thus, if we let $`Z=XW`$
and $`a_{i}=(Z^{\top}Z)^{\dagger}Z^{\top}\hat{y}_{i}`$ (the optimal
task-specific head), then minimizing <a href="#S2.E1" class="ltx_ref"
title="In 2 Preliminaries ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Equation</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">1</span></a> is equivalent to

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\min_{Z=XW}\|Z(Z^{\top}Z)^{\dagger}Z^{\top}\hat{y}_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}\|^{2},
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(3)</span> |

which can be further transformed into

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\max_{P_{Z}}~\hat{y}_{i}^{\top}P_{Z}\hat{y}_{i},\quad P_{Z}=Z(Z^{\top}Z)^{\dagger}Z^{\top}.`$ |  |

Therefore, the original MTL problem can be reformulated into the
following multi-objective problem

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\max_{P_{Z}}\left(\hat{y}_{1}^{\top}P_{Z}\hat{y}_{1},\cdots,\hat{y}_{k}^{\top}P_{Z}\hat{y}_{k}\right),
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(4)</span> |

where $`P_{Z}`$ is a projection matrix with rank at most $`q`$ (i.e.,
hidden layer width) and maps to the column space of $`X`$. Each
quadratic term can be further expressed as $`\|P_{Z}\hat{y}_{i}\|^{2}`$.
From here it is clear (also see <a href="#S2" class="ltx_ref"
title="2 Preliminaries ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">2</span></a>) that when $`q\geq k`$ (the
over-parametrized regime), the $`k`$ quadratic terms can be maximized
simultaneously, meaning that the network has sufficient capacity to fit
the target tasks. In this case, the Pareto front reduces to a
<span id="S3.p2.4.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">singleton</span>,
and can be achieved by scalarization with any choice of convex
coefficients. In fact, this holds true even for general non-linear
networks as long as it is wide enough, i.e., over-parametrized, and we
defer more discussions to <a href="#A1.SS2" class="ltx_ref"
title="A.2 Over-parametrization reduces the Pareto front to a singleton ‣ Appendix A Omitted details from ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">A.2</span></a>.

</div>

<div id="S3.p3" class="ltx_para">

The main focus of our study, in contrast, lies in the
<span id="S3.p3.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">under-parametrized
regime</span> $`q<k`$, where various tasks compete for the network’s
capacity for better prediction. We comment that this regime has been the
focus of previous work and demonstrated to be beneficial in MTL
(<a href="#bib.bib44" class="ltx_ref">Wu et al., 2020</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib43" class="ltx_ref">Wang et al., 2022</a>). We study
two extreme cases within the under-parameterized regime, $`q=1`$ and
$`q=k-1`$. By doing so, we anticipate that our results will hold for
other $`k`$’s in between.

</div>

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="S3.Thmtheorem1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Theorem 3.1</span></span><span id="S3.Thmtheorem1.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="S3.Thmtheorem1.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S3.Thmtheorem1.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">Let

``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(6)</span> |

equal to $`1`$, except for the entries at positions
$`i_{1},\cdots,i_{l}`$ which are equal to $`-1`$. Consider the following
surface parameterized by $`v`$:</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
E_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}:=\left\{v:\sqrt{v}^{\top}Q_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}\sqrt{v}=1\right\}.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(7)</span> |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq k}\bigcup_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{l}\leq k}E_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}},
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(8)</span> |

<span id="S3.Thmtheorem1.p1.4.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">i.e.,
$`\mathcal{F}_{1}`$ is the union of $`2^{k-1}`$ surfaces in the
non-negative orthant (note
$`E_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}=E_{\overline{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}}`$ by
symmetry).</span>

<div id="S3.SS1.p4" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S3.SS1.p4.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Proof sketch
of <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem1" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem 3.1. ‣ 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Theorem</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.1</span></a>.</span> A rank-$`1`$
projection matrix can be expressed as $`ss^{\top}`$ with $`\|s\|=1`$.
The feasible region can therefore be equivalently characterized as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\left\{\left(\langle\hat{y_{1}},s\rangle^{2},\cdots,\langle\hat{y_{k}},s\rangle^{2}\right)^{\top}\bigg|\ \|s\|=1,\ s\in\operatorname{span}(\{\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i\in[k]})\right\}.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(9)</span> |

Define $`v_{i}=\langle\hat{y}_{i},s\rangle`$ and
$`v=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{k})^{\top}`$. We will show the set of $`v`$ forms
the boundary of an ellipsoid determined by $`Q`$. As a consequence, the
set of $`|v|`$ is the union of multiple surfaces, each corresponding to
the boundary of an ellipsoid (reflection of $`Q`$) in the non-negative
orthant. The reflection is represented by the diagonal matrix
$`D_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}`$ in the theorem statement. Finally,
$`\mathcal{F}_{1}`$, which is the set of $`v^{2}`$, can be obtained by
squaring the coordinates in the previous set. This finishes the proof
of <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem1" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem 3.1. ‣ 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Theorem</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.1</span></a>.

</div>

$`(k-1)`$-dimensional subspace, we immediately have the following result
(proof deferred to <a href="#A1.SS4" class="ltx_ref"

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="S3.Thmtheorem2.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Theorem 3.2</span></span><span id="S3.Thmtheorem2.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="S3.Thmtheorem2.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S3.Thmtheorem2.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">Let
$`t:=(\|\hat{y}_{1}\|^{2},\cdots,\|\hat{y}_{k}\|^{2})^{\top}`$. Consider

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
I_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}=\left\{v:\sqrt{t-v}^{\top}Q_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}\sqrt{t-v}=1\right\}.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(10)</span> |

<span id="S3.Thmtheorem2.p1.2.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">We

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |

``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(11)</span> |

<span id="S3.Thmtheorem2.p1.3.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">i.e.,
$`\mathcal{F}_{k-1}`$ is the union of $`2^{k-1}`$ surfaces in the
non-negative orthant
($`I_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}=I_{\overline{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}}`$ by
symmetry).</span>

</div>

<div id="S3.SS1.p6" class="ltx_para">

<a href="#S3.Thmtheorem1" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem 3.1. ‣ 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Theorems</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.1</span></a>
and <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem2" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem 3.2. ‣ 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.2</span></a> not only offer us a glimpse
into the shape of the feasible region but also provide valuable insights
into the fundamental limitation of linear scalarization. The key
observation is that, scalarization fails to reach the
<span id="S3.SS1.p6.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">intersection</span> points of two (or
more) surfaces when the gradients with respect to the two (or more)
surfaces disagree (i.e., they lie in different directions). We refer to
this phenomenon as <span id="S3.SS1.p6.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">gradient disagreement</span>, and
provide an illustrating example in <a href="#S3.F2" class="ltx_ref"
title="In 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Figure</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">2</span></a>. Essentially, the
multi-surface structure that we have revealed implies the abundance of
intersection points on the feasible region $`\mathcal{F}_{q}`$.
Moreover, if any of these points were to be an PO, scalarization would
not be able to trace out the Pareto front
(see <a href="#A1.SS5" class="ltx_ref"
title="A.5 Why scalarization fails in the presence of gradient disagreement ‣ Appendix A Omitted details from ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">A.5</span></a> for a detailed explanation).

</div>

<div id="S3.SS1.p7" class="ltx_para">

We illustrate this observation using a concrete example with three tasks
in <a href="#S3.F1" class="ltx_ref"
title="In 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Figure</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">1</span></a>. The feasible region consists
of four surfaces, among which $`E_{\varnothing}`$ is dominated by other
surfaces. The intersection of $`E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}`$ (the red point) is a
PO that achieves balanced performance across tasks, yet scalarization
cannot reach this point since it lies in a valley.

</div>

</div>

<div id="S3.SS2" class="section ltx_subsection">

### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_subsection">3.2 </span>Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration

<div id="S3.SS2.p1" class="ltx_para">

In this section, we provide an in-depth analysis of the Pareto front,
which is a subset of the feasible region. Concretely, we develop
necessary and sufficient conditions for scalarization to fully explore
the Pareto front.

</div>

<div id="S3.SS2.p2" class="ltx_para">

We first review some important concepts that will be frequently used in
this section. Denote
$`\mathcal{Q}:=\{Q_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}\mid 0\leq l\leq k,\ 1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{l}\leq k\}`$
as the collection of matrices which define the surfaces in the previous
section. Denote
$`\mathcal{G}:=\{G_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}=Q_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}^{-1}\mid 0\leq l\leq k,\ 1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{l}\leq k\}`$.
Note
$`G_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}=D_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}\hat{Y}^{\top}\hat{Y}D_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}=(\hat{Y}D_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}})^{\top}(\hat{Y}D_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}})`$.
Therefore, $`\mathcal{G}`$ is a collection of gram matrices which
measure the <span id="S3.SS2.p2.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">task similarity</span>, and each
element is obtained by flipping the signs of some optimal predictors. We
use $`\mathcal{E}`$ and $`\mathcal{I}`$ to denote the collection of
surfaces defined in <a href="#S3.E7" class="ltx_ref"
title="In Theorem 3.1. ‣ 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Equations</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">7</span></a>
and <a href="#S3.E10" class="ltx_ref"
title="Equation 10 ‣ Theorem 3.2. ‣ 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">10</span></a>, respectively. We also
formally define a class of matrix as follows.

</div>

<div id="S3.Thmtheorem3" class="ltx_theorem ltx_theorem_definition">

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="S3.Thmtheorem3.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Definition 3.3</span></span><span id="S3.Thmtheorem3.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold"> </span>(Doubly non-negative matrix)<span id="S3.Thmtheorem3.4" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="S3.Thmtheorem3.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S3.Thmtheorem3.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">A
doubly non-negative matrix $`M`$ is a real positive semi-definite (PSD)
matrix with non-negative entries.</span>

</div>

</div>

<div id="S3.SS2.p3" class="ltx_para">

Our analysis begins with an observation that the distribution of PO
across different surfaces plays a vital role in the success of
scalarization. Specifically, we want to avoid the “bad” case like the
one demonstrated in <a href="#S3.F1" class="ltx_ref"
title="In 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Figure</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">1</span></a>, where a PO lies on the
intersection of multiple surfaces. A natural solution to circumvent this
issue is to force the Pareto front to lie on a <span id="S3.SS2.p3.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">single</span> surface. We therefore
draw our attention to this particular scenario, and provide a necessary
and sufficient condition for such configuration in the following lemma.

</div>

<div id="S3.Thmtheorem4" class="ltx_theorem ltx_theorem_lemma">

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="S3.Thmtheorem4.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Lemma 3.4</span></span><span id="S3.Thmtheorem4.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="S3.Thmtheorem4.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S3.Thmtheorem4.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">When
$`q=1`$, the Pareto front of the feasible region belongs to a single
surface $`E^{*}\in\mathcal{E}`$, if and only if the corresponding
$`G^{*}=(Q^{*})^{-1}\in\mathcal{G}`$ is doubly non-negative. We refer to
the existence of such a doubly non-negative $`G^{*}`$ in $`\mathcal{G}`$
as <span id="S3.Thmtheorem4.p1.1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_typewriter">C1</span> <span id="c1"
class="ltx_anchor"></span> .</span>

</div>

</div>

<div id="S3.SS2.p4" class="ltx_para">

We outline the proof sketch below. The detailed proof can be found
in <a href="#A2.SS1" class="ltx_ref"
title="B.1 Proof of ‣ Appendix B Omitted details from ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">B.1</span></a>.

</div>

<div id="S3.SS2.p5" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S3.SS2.p5.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Proof sketch
of <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem4" class="ltx_ref"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Lemma</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.4</span></a>.</span> For the
<span id="S3.SS2.p5.1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">if</span>
part, we employ a geometric argument. Specifically, denote the acute
cone formed by the optimal predictors as $`\mathcal{A}`$. We will show
that, if $`s`$ (a unit vector in <a href="#S3.E9" class="ltx_ref"
title="In 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Equation</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">9</span></a>) does not belong to the dual
cone $`\mathcal{A}^{*}`$, then its reflection w.r.t. the dual cone
$`s^{\prime}`$ is a better point than $`s`$. As a consequence, we can
remove the absolute value from the proof sketch
of <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem1" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem 3.1. ‣ 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Theorem</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.1</span></a>. This further implies that
the Pareto front belongs to a single surface.

</div>

<div id="S3.SS2.p6" class="ltx_para">

For the <span id="S3.SS2.p6.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">only
if</span> part, we consider the points which achieve maximum value along
an axis. We will show these points are PO. Finally, we will show that if
<a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a>
does not hold, then these points must belong to different surfaces in
$`\mathcal{E}`$.

</div>

<div id="S3.SS2.p7" class="ltx_para">

We have a similar result for $`q=k-1`$. The details of the proof are
deferred to <a href="#A2.SS2" class="ltx_ref"
title="B.2 Proof of ‣ Appendix B Omitted details from ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">B.2</span></a>.

</div>

<div id="S3.Thmtheorem5" class="ltx_theorem ltx_theorem_lemma">

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="S3.Thmtheorem5.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Lemma 3.5</span></span><span id="S3.Thmtheorem5.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="S3.Thmtheorem5.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S3.Thmtheorem5.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">When
$`q=k-1`$, the Pareto front of the feasible region belongs to a single
surface $`I^{*}\in\mathcal{I}`$, if and only if the corresponding
$`Q^{*}\in\mathcal{Q}`$ is doubly non-negative. We refer to the
existence of such a doubly non-negative $`Q^{*}`$ in $`\mathcal{Q}`$ as
<span id="S3.Thmtheorem5.p1.1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_typewriter">C2</span> <span id="c2"
class="ltx_anchor"></span> .</span>

</div>

</div>

<div id="S3.SS2.p8" class="ltx_para">

<a href="#S3.Thmtheorem4" class="ltx_ref"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Lemmas</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.4</span></a>
and <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem5" class="ltx_ref"

class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.5</span></a> provide a refined
characterization of how the PO is distributed within the feasible
region: unless <a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"

holds, the Pareto front will belong to multiple surfaces in
$`\mathcal{E}`$ (resp. $`\mathcal{I}`$). This easily leads to the
existence of a PO on the intersection of different surfaces
(see <a href="#S3.F1" class="ltx_ref"
title="In 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Figure</span> <span

us to conjecture that <a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a>
(resp. <a href="#c2" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"

is necessary for full exploration. We make the following assumption
regarding the topology of the Pareto front.

</div>

<div id="S3.Thmtheorem6" class="ltx_theorem ltx_theorem_assumption">

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="S3.Thmtheorem6.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Assumption 3.6</span></span><span id="S3.Thmtheorem6.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="S3.Thmtheorem6.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S3.Thmtheorem6.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">For
$`q=1`$ and $`q=k-1`$, we assume the Pareto front is path-connected,
i.e., for every pair of PO $`x`$ and $`y`$, there exists a continuous
curve on the Pareto front with $`x`$ and $`y`$ being the two
endpoints.</span>

</div>

</div>

<div id="S3.SS2.p9" class="ltx_para">

<a href="#S3.Thmtheorem6" class="ltx_ref"

<a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"

</div>

<div id="S3.Thmtheorem7" class="ltx_theorem ltx_theorem_assumption">

<div id="S3.Thmtheorem7.p1" class="ltx_para">

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">4 </span>Discussion

<div id="S4.p1" class="ltx_para">

We discuss the implications of our theoretical results, and provide a
remedy solution for linear scalarization to address its inability of
full exploration.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1" class="section ltx_subsection">

### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_subsection">4.1 </span>Implications of the theoretical results

<div id="S4.SS1.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S4.SS1.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Fundamental
limitation of linear
scalarization.</span> From <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem8" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem 3.8 (Necessity of C1 (resp. C2)). ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Theorem</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.8</span></a>, it is clear that linear
scalarization faces significant challenges in fully exploring the Pareto
front in the case of $`q=1`$ and $`q=k-1`$, since both
<a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a>
and <a href="#c2" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.5. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C2</a>
require the existence of a doubly non-negative matrix, which is a very
small subset of the PSD matrix. To illustrate this point, we consider a
simplified probabilistic model: when the optimal predictors
$`\{\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}`$ are drawn independently from a symmetric
distribution centered at the origin, the probability that
<a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a>
holds is $`2^{-O(k^{2})}`$, which decays exponentially with the number
of tasks. Thus, we comment that
<a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a>
and <a href="#c2" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.5. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C2</a>
in general does not hold in practical cases.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1.p2" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S4.SS1.p2.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Towards
understanding when and why scalarization fails.</span>  Among the vast
literature that studies Pareto optimality in MTL, there is a noticeable
absence of a systematic and rigorous exploration of
<span id="S4.SS1.p2.1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">when and why
scalarization fails to reach a Pareto optimum</span>. Prior work
typically resorts to hypothetical examples (e.g., Figure 4.9
in (<a href="#bib.bib2" class="ltx_ref">Boyd et al., 2004</a>)) to
demonstrate the failure of scalarization. Our study, which identifies
the multi-surface structure of the feasible region and reveals the
phenomenon of gradient disagreement in a concrete setting, serves as a
pioneering step in addressing this gap in the literature.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1.p3" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S4.SS1.p3.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">A closer
examination of the failure mode.</span> For the extreme
under-parametrized case ($`q=1`$), on the one hand, we can prove that
scalarization is able to achieve points which attain maximum value along
one direction (i.e., best performance on one task). On the other hand,
by examining <a href="#S3.F1" class="ltx_ref"
title="In 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Figure</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">1</span></a>, we shall see that
scalarization is incapable of exploring the interior points (i.e.,
balanced performance across all tasks). This suggests that scalarization
has the tendency to <span id="S4.SS1.p3.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">overfit</span> to a small fraction of
tasks, and fails to strike the right balance between different tasks. We
provide more empirical evidence to support this argument
in <a href="#S5" class="ltx_ref"
title="5 Experiments ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">5</span></a>.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1.p4" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S4.SS1.p4.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Criteria for
practitioners.</span>  <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem10" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem 3.10 (Sufficiency of C1 (resp. C2)). ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Theorem</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.10</span></a> demonstrates that
<a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a>
and <a href="#c2" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.5. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C2</a>
are sufficient for scalarization to fully explore the Pareto front. As
such, these conditions can serve as valuable criteria for practitioners
when deciding which tasks to combine in linear MTL. Note a naive
enumeration of $`\mathcal{G}`$ or $`\mathcal{Q}`$ will lead to a time
complexity of $`O(2^{k})`$. We instead provide an $`O(k^{2})`$ algorithm
checking whether <a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a>
holds (<a href="#alg1" class="ltx_ref"
title="In Appendix C A polynomial algorithm for checking C1 ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Algorithm</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">1</span></a>
in <a href="#A3" class="ltx_ref"
title="Appendix C A polynomial algorithm for checking C1 ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Appendix</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">C</span></a>). In essence, the algorithm
aims to find a set of flipping signs $`\{s_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}\in\{+,-\}`$
that ensure positive or zero correlation between all pairs in
$`\{s_{i}\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}`$. It scans through the predictors in
ascending order and either determines the signs $`s_{j}`$ for each
optimal predictor $`\hat{y}_{j}`$ if not determined yet, or checks for
conflicts between signs that are already determined. A sign can be
determined when the predictors $`\hat{y}_{i}`$ and $`\hat{y}_{j}`$ are
correlated. A conflict indicates the impossibility of finding such a set
of flipping signs, thus falsifying
<a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a>.
A similar algorithm can be devised for
<a href="#c2" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.5. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C2</a>
with an additional step of Cholesky decomposition beforehand, leading to
an $`O(k^{3})`$ time complexity.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1.p5" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S4.SS1.p5.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Open questions
in <a href="#bib.bib46" class="ltx_ref">Xin et al. (2022)</a>.</span> We
(partially) answer the open questions
in <a href="#bib.bib46" class="ltx_ref">Xin et al. (2022)</a>.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1.p6" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S4.SS1.p6.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_typewriter">Q1</span>.
“Is it the case that neural networks trained via a combination of
scalarization and standard first-order optimization methods are not able
to reach the Pareto Frontier?”

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1.p7" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S4.SS1.p7.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_typewriter">A1</span>.
For Linear MTL, our answer is a definitive “Yes”. We demonstrate that
linear scalarization with arbitrary convex coefficients cannot trace out
all the Pareto optima in general. Our results are strong in the sense
that they hold on a <span id="S4.SS1.p7.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">representation</span> level, meaning
that they are independent of the specific optimization algorithm
employed. This suggests that the weakness we have revealed in
scalarization is fundamental in nature.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1.p8" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S4.SS1.p8.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_typewriter">Q2</span>.
“Is non-convexity adding additional complexity which makes scalarization
insufficient for tracing out the Pareto front?”

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1.p9" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S4.SS1.p9.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_typewriter">A2</span>.
Yes, even some mild non-convexity introduced by two-layer linear
networks will render scalarization insufficient. On the other hand, we
do believe the multi-surface structure that we have unveiled
in <a href="#S3.SS1" class="ltx_ref"
title="3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.1</span></a> is both universal and
fundamental, which also contributes to the observed non-convexity in
practical scenarios. In essence, what we have done partly is to
penetrate through the surface and gain a holistic view of the feasible
region. Notably, the key obstacle in linear scalarization, as revealed
by our analysis, lies in the phenomenon of <span id="S4.SS1.p9.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">gradient disagreement</span>. This
cannot be readily discerned by solely examining the surface.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS1.p10" class="ltx_para">

We make two additional comments to further clarify our standing point in
comparison to this work.

1.  <span id="S4.I1.i1"><span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">1.</span></span>
    <div id="S4.I1.i1.p1" class="ltx_para">

    We do not aim to refute the claims
    in <a href="#bib.bib46" class="ltx_ref">Xin et al. (2022)</a>; as a
    matter of fact, we consider their empirical findings highly
    valuable. Instead, we would like to emphasize a discrepancy between
    their theory and experiments. Given that we have shown the
    conclusion—that linear scalarization can fully explore the PF—no
    long holds in the non-convex setting, their theory does not account
    for their experiments on modern neural networks. Therefore, we urge
    the research community to develop a new theory elucidating the
    empirical efficacy of linear scalarization.

    </div>
2.  <span id="S4.I1.i2"><span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">2.</span></span>
    <div id="S4.I1.i2.p1" class="ltx_para">

    While the differing settings render the results from these two
    papers non-comparable, they collectively offer a broader perspective
    on the pros and cons of scalarization and SMTOs. We believe this is
    of great significance to both researchers and practitioners.

    </div>

</div>

</div>

<div id="S4.SS2" class="section ltx_subsection">

### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_subsection">4.2 </span>A remedy solution

<div id="S4.SS2.p1" class="ltx_para">

Given the fundamental weakness of linear scalarization in fully
exploring the Pareto front, one might wonder whether there exists any
model class where linear scalarization is sufficient to fully explore
the Pareto front. The answer is affirmative if we allow ourselves to use
*randomized multi-task neural networks*. More specifically, given two
MTL networks $`f^{(0)}`$ and $`f^{(1)}`$ over the same input and output
spaces and a fixed constant $`t\in[0,1]`$, we can construct a randomized
MTL network as follows. Let $`S\sim U(0,1)`$ be a uniform random
variable over $`(0,1)`$ that is independent of all the other random
variables, such as the inputs. Then, consider the following randomized
neural network:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{cases}f^{(0)}(x)&\mathrm{if~}S\leq t\\
f^{(1)}(x)&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{cases}
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(12)</span> |

Essentially, the randomized network constructed
by <a href="#S4.E12" class="ltx_ref"
title="In 4.2 A remedy solution ‣ 4 Discussion ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Equation</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">12</span></a> will output $`f^{(0)}(x)`$
with probability $`t`$ and $`f^{(1)}(x)`$ with probability $`1-t`$. Now
if we consider the loss of $`f(\cdot)`$, we have

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\mathbb{E}_{S}[\|f(X)-Y\|_{2}^{2}]`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|f(X)-Y\|_{2}^{2}]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=t\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|f(X)-Y\|_{2}^{2}\mid S\leq t]+(1-t)\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|f(X)-Y\|_{2}^{2}\mid S>t]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=t\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|f^{(0)}(X)-Y\|_{2}^{2}]+(1-t)\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|f^{(1)}(X)-Y\|_{2}^{2}],`$ |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(13)</span> |

which means that the loss of the randomized multi-task network is a
convex combination of the losses from $`f^{(0)}(\cdot)`$ and
$`f^{(1)}(\cdot)`$. Geometrically, the argument above shows that
randomization allows us to “convexify” the feasible region and thus the
Pareto front will be convex. Now, by a standard application of the
supporting hyperplane
theorem (<a href="#bib.bib2" class="ltx_ref">Boyd et al., 2004</a>,
Section 2.5.2) we can see that every point on the Pareto front can be
realized via linear scalarization. We empirically verify the
effectiveness of randomization in <a href="#A4.SS3" class="ltx_ref"
title="D.3 Additional experiments on randomization ‣ Appendix D Additional experimental details and results ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">D.3</span></a>.

</div>

<div id="S4.SS2.p2" class="ltx_para">

We comment that randomization is a powerful tool that has wide
applicability in machine learning. For instance, it has achieved great
success in online learning, specifically in the
Follow-the-Perturbed-Leader
algorithm (<a href="#bib.bib16" class="ltx_ref">Kalai and Vempala, 2005</a>).
It also appears in the literature of fairness to achieve the optimal
regressor or
classifier (<a href="#bib.bib54" class="ltx_ref">Zhao and Gordon, 2022</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib45" class="ltx_ref">Xian et al., 2023</a>). Here we
further demonstrate that it can be applied to MTL to facilitate the
exploration of linear scalarization. In essence, randomization increases
the representation power of the original neural network.

</div>

</div>

</div>

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">5 </span>Experiments

<div id="S5.p1" class="ltx_para">

In this section, we corroborate our theoretical findings with a linear
MTL experiment, where we show that linear scalarization fails to fully
explore the Pareto front on a three-task problem that does not satisfy
<a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a>
with $`q=1`$. In comparison, SMTOs can achieve balanced Pareto optimum
solutions that are not achievable by scalarization.

</div>

<div id="S5.p2" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S5.p2.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Dataset and
setup.</span> We use the SARCOS dataset for our
experiment (<a href="#bib.bib42" class="ltx_ref">Vijayakumar and Schaal, 2000</a>),
where the problem is to predict the torque of seven robot arms given
inputs that consist of the position, velocity, and acceleration of the
respective arms. For ease of visualization, we restrict the tasks to
arms 3, 4, and 5; in particular, they do not satisfy
<a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a>.

</div>

<div id="S5.p3" class="ltx_para">

Our regression model is a two-layer linear network with hidden size
$`q=1`$ (no bias). To explore the portion of the Pareto front achievable
by linear scalarization, we fit 100,000 linear regressors with randomly
sampled convex coefficients and record their performance. These are
compared to the solutions found by SMTOs, for which we use
MGDA (<a href="#bib.bib9" class="ltx_ref">Désidéri, 2012</a>) and
MGDA-UB (<a href="#bib.bib37" class="ltx_ref">Sener and Koltun, 2018</a>).
Specifically, to guarantee their Pareto optimality, we run the SMTOs
with full-batch gradient descent till convergence (hence each SMTO
method has one deterministic solution). Additional details, including
hyperparameter settings, can be found
in <a href="#A4.SS1" class="ltx_ref"
title="D.1 Additional experimental details ‣ Appendix D Additional experimental details and results ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">D.1</span></a>.

</div>

<div id="S5.p4" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S5.p4.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Results.</span> In
<a href="#S5.F3" class="ltx_ref"
title="In 5 Experiments ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span

class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3</span></a>, we plot the MSEs achieved by
scalarization under randomly sampled weights. We observe that the
solutions achieved by scalarization are concentrated near the vertices
and partly along the edges, indicating their tendency to overfit to 1–2
of the tasks. Scalarization also fails to explore a large portion of the
Pareto front in the center—which have more balanced performance across
task; the inability of full exploration corroborates the necessary
condition (<a href="#S3.Thmtheorem8" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem 3.8 (Necessity of C1 (resp. C2)). ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span

capable of finding solutions not achievable by scalarization that are
located in the center of the Pareto front. This showcases the relative
advantage and practical utility of SMTOs in MTL over scalarization,
especially in scenarios where equitable performance on all tasks are
desired. To strengthen our results, we experiment with multiple random
initializations and observe consistent results
(see <a href="#A4.SS2" class="ltx_ref"
title="D.2 Additional experiments on random initialization ‣ Appendix D Additional experimental details and results ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">D.2</span></a>).

</div>

<div id="S5.p5" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S5.p5.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Discussion.</span> We would like to note
that we are not endorsing any specific SMTO algorithm, such as MGDA or
MGDA-UB. As a matter of fact, we hypothesize that no single algorithm is
universally effective (also
see (<a href="#bib.bib4" class="ltx_ref">Chen et al., 2023</a>)),
notably in overcoming the limitation of scalarization in terms of full
exploration. Instead, by revealing the limitation of scalarization and
the potential benefits of <span id="S5.p5.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">some</span> SMTO algorithms, we aim to
strengthen SMTO research, challenge the notion that linear scalarization
is sufficient, and advocate for a more balanced progression in the field
of MTL.

</div>

</div>

<div id="S6" class="section ltx_section">

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">6 </span>Limitations and future directions

<div id="S6.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S6.p1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Limitations.</span> There are two major
limitations of our work. First, while the techniques we develop in this
paper are highly non-trivial, we only cover the two extreme cases
$`q=1`$ and $`q=k-1`$ in the under-parametrized regime, and the analysis
for general $`q<k`$ is missing. Second, the setting of our study is
relatively restricted. Specifically, we focus on linear MTL where
different tasks share the same input. A more realistic setting should
factor in different inputs for different
tasks (<a href="#bib.bib44" class="ltx_ref">Wu et al., 2020</a>), as
well as some non-linearity in the model
architecture (<a href="#bib.bib18" class="ltx_ref">Kurin et al., 2022</a>).

</div>

<div id="S6.p2" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S6.p2.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Future
directions.</span> We expect the geometry of the feasible region to
transition smoothly as we increase $`q`$, meaning that it will not
abruptly collapse into a <span id="S6.p2.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">single</span> surface. Providing a
rigorous basis for this assertion would facilitate the analysis for
general $`q`$’s, thereby complementing our study. Regarding the
assumption of shared input, while our analysis persists under the
‘proportional covariance assumption’ adopted
by <a href="#bib.bib44" class="ltx_ref">Wu et al. (2020)</a>, we find
this assumption excessively restrictive, and hope future work could
relax it further. The generalization to neural networks with ReLU
activation is interesting but also challenging, and it is unclear
whether the techniques and results developed in this paper can be
extended to the non-linear setting. Another interesting avenue to
explore is interpreting the multi-surface structure we have uncovered,
which we hypothesize to have connections with game theory. Finally, our
paper dedicates to demonstrating the weaknesses of linear scalarization,
while we consider it an important future direction to theoretically
analyze the advantage of SMTOs, as partially revealed in our
experiments. In all, we hope our study can initiate a line of works on
MTL, which provide theoretical justifications for the usage of specific
algorithms beyond empirical evaluations across various settings.

</div>

</div>

<div id="S7" class="section ltx_section">

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">7 </span>Related work

<div id="S7.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S7.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">SMTOs.</span> There
is a surge of interest in developing specialized multi-task optimizers
(SMTOs) in the literature. One of the earliest and most notable SMTOs is
the multiple-gradient descent algorithm
(MGDA) (<a href="#bib.bib9" class="ltx_ref">Désidéri, 2012</a>). For
each task, MGDA solves an optimization problem and performs updates
using the gradient of the objective function with both shared and
task-specific parameters. However, MGDA cannot scale to high dimension
(e.g., neural networks), and computing the gradients for every task is
prohibitively expensive. To counter these
drawbacks, <a href="#bib.bib37" class="ltx_ref">Sener and Koltun (2018)</a>
introduce a scalable Frank-Wolfe-based optimizer which is more
efficient. Another prominent issue in MTL is that the gradients from
multiple tasks could conflict with each other and the performance of
some tasks could be significantly affected. To this end,
<a href="#bib.bib49" class="ltx_ref">Yu et al. (2020b)</a> propose
<span id="S7.p1.1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">gradient
surgery</span> to mitigate the negative interference among tasks;
<a href="#bib.bib5" class="ltx_ref">Chen et al. (2020)</a>
and <a href="#bib.bib23" class="ltx_ref">Liu et al. (2021a)</a> propose
to manipulate the gradients or the gradient selection procedures;
<a href="#bib.bib12" class="ltx_ref">Fernando et al. (2022)</a> devise a
stochastic gradient correction method and prove its convergence. More
recently, <a href="#bib.bib33" class="ltx_ref">Navon et al. (2022)</a>
cast the gradient update as a Nash bargaining game, yielding
well-balanced solutions across the PF.

</div>

<div id="S7.p2" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S7.p2.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Empirical comparison
of SMTOs and scalarization.</span>  A recent line of works empirically
compare SMTOs and linear scalarization. Their central claim is that the
performance of linear scalarization is comparable with the
state-of-the-art SMTOs despite the added complexity of SMTOs.
<a href="#bib.bib46" class="ltx_ref">Xin et al. (2022)</a> argue that
multi-task optimization methods do not yield any improvement beyond what
can be achieved by linear scalarization with carefully-tuned
hyperparameters;
<a href="#bib.bib18" class="ltx_ref">Kurin et al. (2022)</a> show that
the performance of linear scalarization matches or even surpasses the
more complex SMTOs when combined with standard regularization
techniques. In addition to classification
tasks, <a href="#bib.bib40" class="ltx_ref">Vandenhende et al. (2021)</a>
conduct extensive experiments on dense prediction tasks, i.e., tasks
that produce pixel-level predictions. They conclude that avoiding
gradient competition among tasks can actually lead to performance
degradation, and linear scalarization with fixed weights outperforms
some SMTOs.

</div>

<div id="S7.p3" class="ltx_para">

<span id="S7.p3.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Exploration of the
Pareto front.</span>  It is well-known that minimizing scalarization
objectives with positive coefficients yields Pareto optimal
solutions (<a href="#bib.bib2" class="ltx_ref">Boyd et al., 2004</a>),
while using non-negative coefficients yields weak Pareto optimal
ones (<a href="#bib.bib50" class="ltx_ref">Zadeh, 1963</a>). On the
other hand, exploration of the <span id="S7.p3.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">entire</span> Pareto front has been a
long-standing and difficult open problem in MTL. For the simplest case,
if all loss functions are convex or the achievable region is a convex
set, then scalarization can fully explore the PF
(<a href="#bib.bib2" class="ltx_ref">Boyd et al., 2004</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib46" class="ltx_ref">Xin et al., 2022</a>). A slightly
weaker condition for full exploration is directional
convexity (<a href="#bib.bib15" class="ltx_ref">Holtzman and Halkin, 1966</a>),
established by <a href="#bib.bib20" class="ltx_ref">Lin (1976)</a>.
Several recent works in MTL develop algorithms to generate PO subject to
user preferences, in particular on trade-offs among certain
tasks (<a href="#bib.bib21" class="ltx_ref">Lin et al., 2019</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib27" class="ltx_ref">Mahapatra and Rajan, 2020</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib31" class="ltx_ref">Momma et al., 2022</a>). There is
also a line of empirical works that focuses on designing algorithms to
efficiently explore the
PF (<a href="#bib.bib26" class="ltx_ref">Ma et al., 2020</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib22" class="ltx_ref">Lin et al., 2020</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib25" class="ltx_ref">Liu et al., 2021c</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib32" class="ltx_ref">Navon et al., 2021</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib35" class="ltx_ref">Ruchte and Grabocka, 2021</a>;
<a href="#bib.bib47" class="ltx_ref">Ye and Liu, 2022</a>). As far as we
know, we are the first to establish both necessary and sufficient
conditions for full exploration in the non-convex setting.

</div>

</div>

<div id="S8" class="section ltx_section">

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">8 </span>Conclusion

<div id="S8.p1" class="ltx_para">

In this paper, we revisit linear scalarization from a theoretical
perspective. In contrast to recent works that claimed consistent
empirical advantages of scalarization, we show its inherent limitation
in fully exploring the Pareto front. Specifically, we reveal a
multi-surface structure of the feasible region, and identify necessary
and sufficient conditions for full exploration in the under-parametrized
regime. We also discuss several implications of our theoretical results.
Experiments on a real-world dataset corroborate our theoretical
findings, and suggest the benefit of SMTOs in finding balanced
solutions.

</div>

</div>

<div id="A1.SS2" class="section ltx_subsection">

### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_subsection">A.2 </span>Over-parametrization reduces the Pareto front to a singleton

<div id="A1.SS2.p1" class="ltx_para">

For general non-linear multi-task neural networks, we will show that
increasing the width reduces the Pareto front to a singleton, where all
tasks simultaneously achieve zero training loss. As a consequence,
linear scalarization with any convex coefficients will be able to
achieve this point.

</div>

<div id="A1.SS2.p2" class="ltx_para">

To illustrate this point, we follow the same setting as
in <a href="#S2" class="ltx_ref"
title="2 Preliminaries ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">2</span></a>, except changing the model to
be a two-layer ReLU multi-task network with bias terms. Concretely, for
task $`i`$, the prediction on input $`x`$ is given by
$`f_{i}(x,W,b,a_{i})=a_{i}^{\top}\max(Wx+b,0)`$. We have the following
result.

</div>

<div id="A1.Thmtheorem1" class="ltx_theorem ltx_theorem_theorem">

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="A1.Thmtheorem1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Theorem A.1</span></span><span id="A1.Thmtheorem1.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="A1.Thmtheorem1.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A1.Thmtheorem1.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">There
exists a two-layer ReLU multi-task network with hidden width $`q=nk`$
and parameters $`(W,b,a_{1},\cdots,a_{k})`$, such that</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle f_{i}(x_{i}^{j},W,b,a_{i})=y_{i}^{j},\quad\forall j\in[n],\ \forall i\in[k].`$ |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(14)</span> |

<span id="A1.Thmtheorem1.p1.2.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">This
implies that the network achieves zero training loss on each
task.</span>

</div>

</div>

<div id="A1.SS2.2" class="ltx_proof">

###### Proof.

<div id="A1.SS2.p3" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A1.SS2.p3.1.1" class="ltx_text">For every $`i\in[k]`$, we can
apply Theorem 1
in \[<a href="#bib.bib51" class="ltx_ref">Zhang et al., 2021</a>\] and
find $`(W_{i},b_{i},\tilde{a}_{i})`$ such that</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{i}^{\top}\max(W_{i}x_{i}^{j}+b_{i},0)=y_{i}^{j},\quad\forall j\in[n].`$ |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(15)</span> |

<span id="A1.SS2.p3.2.1" class="ltx_text">Here,
$`W_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}`$ and
$`b_{i},\tilde{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}`$. Now consider</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle W=\begin{pmatrix}W_{1}\\
W_{2}\\
\vdots\\
W_{n}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{nk\times p},\quad b=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\\
\vdots\\
b_{n}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{nk},\quad a_{i}=e_{i}\otimes\tilde{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{nk}\quad\forall i\in[n],`$ |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(16)</span> |

<span id="A1.SS2.p3.3.1" class="ltx_text">where $`e_{i}`$ denotes the
$`i`$-th unit vector in $`\mathbb{R}^{n}`$ and $`\otimes`$ stands for
the Kronecker product. It is straightforward to see that</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{a}_{i}^{\top}\max(W_{i}x_{i}^{j}+b_{i},0)=y_{i}^{j},\quad\forall j\in[n],\ \forall i\in[k].`$ |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(17)</span> |

<span id="A1.SS2.p3.4.1" class="ltx_text">This finishes the proof as
desired. ∎</span>

</div>

</div>

</div>

<div id="A1.SS3" class="section ltx_subsection">

### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_subsection">A.3 </span>Proof of <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem1" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem 3.1. ‣ 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Theorem</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.1</span></a>

<div id="A1.SS3.2" class="ltx_proof">

###### Proof of <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem1" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem 3.1. ‣ 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Theorem</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.1</span></a>

<div id="A1.SS3.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A1.SS3.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text">Define
$`v_{i}=\langle\hat{y}_{i},s\rangle`$ and
$`v=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{k})^{\top}`$. We are interested in the set</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
S:=\left\{v\mid\|s\|=1,s\in\operatorname{span}(\{\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i\in[k]})\right\}.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(18)</span> |

<span id="A1.SS3.p1.2.1" class="ltx_text">We will show $`S`$ is
equivalent to the following set</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
B:=\left\{v\mid v^{\top}Qv=1\right\},\quad Q=(\hat{Y}^{\top}\hat{Y})^{-1},
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(19)</span> |

<span id="A1.SS3.p1.3.1" class="ltx_text">which is essentially the
boundary of an ellipsoid.</span>

</div>

<div id="A1.SS3.p2" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A1.SS3.p2.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Step 1:
$`S\subset B`$.<span id="A1.SS3.p2.1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_medium">  Note $`v=\hat{Y}^{\top}s`$, so it
suffices to show</span></span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
s^{\top}\hat{Y}(\hat{Y}^{\top}\hat{Y})^{-1}\hat{Y}^{\top}s=1.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(20)</span> |

<span id="A1.SS3.p2.2.1" class="ltx_text">Denote
$`P_{\hat{Y}}=\hat{Y}(\hat{Y}^{\top}\hat{Y})^{-1}\hat{Y}^{\top}`$, which
is a projection matrix that maps to the column space of $`\hat{Y}`$.
Since $`s\in\operatorname{span}(\{\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i\in[k]})`$, we
have</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
s^{\top}P_{\hat{Y}}s=s^{\top}s=\|s\|^{2}=1.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(21)</span> |

</div>

<div id="A1.SS3.p3" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A1.SS3.p3.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Step 2:
$`B\subset S`$.<span id="A1.SS3.p3.1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_medium">  Since $`\hat{Y}^{\top}`$ has full row
rank, for every $`v\in\mathbb{R}^{k}`$, we can always find
$`s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}`$ such that $`\hat{Y}^{\top}s=v`$. We can further
assume $`s\in\operatorname{span}(\{\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i\in[k]})`$ by
removing the component in the null space of $`\hat{Y}^{\top}`$. Assuming
$`v\in B`$, we have</span></span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
s^{\top}P_{\hat{Y}}s=1,\quad s\in\operatorname{span}(\{\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}).
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(22)</span> |

<span id="A1.SS3.p3.2.1" class="ltx_text">Since $`P_{\hat{Y}}`$ is a
projection matrix that maps to the column space of $`\hat{Y}`$, we have
$`s^{\top}s=1`$, which implies $`\|s\|=1`$.</span>

</div>

<div id="A1.SS3.p4" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A1.SS3.p4.1.1" class="ltx_text">Now we have $`S=B`$. The
set</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
S^{\prime}:=\left\{|v|\mid\|s\|=1,s\in\operatorname{span}(\{\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i\in[k]})\right\}
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(23)</span> |

<span id="A1.SS3.p4.2.1" class="ltx_text">can be obtained by reflecting
$`E`$ to the non-negative orthant. Formally, a reflection is determined
by a collection of axes $`\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{l}\}`$ ($`0\leq l\leq k`$).
The image of such reflection in the non-negative orthant is given
by</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
B_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}=\{v\mid v^{\top}Q_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}v=1,v\geq 0\},
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(24)</span> |

<span id="A1.SS3.p4.3.1" class="ltx_text">where
$`Q_{i_{1},\cdots i_{l}}=D_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}QD_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}`$.
As a consequence, we have</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
S^{\prime}=\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq k}\bigcup_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{l}\leq k}B_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(25)</span> |

<span id="A1.SS3.p4.4.1" class="ltx_text">Finally, the feasible region
can be equivalently characterized as</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\left\{v^{2}\mid\|s\|=1,s\in\operatorname{span}(\{\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i\in[k]})\right\},
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(26)</span> |

<span id="A1.SS3.p4.5.1" class="ltx_text">which can be obtained by
squaring the coordinates of $`S^{\prime}`$. Therefore, if we
denote</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
E_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}=\{{v}\mid\sqrt{v}^{\top}Q_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}\sqrt{v}=1\},
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(27)</span> |

<span id="A1.SS3.p4.6.1" class="ltx_text">then (note the square root
naturally implies $`v\geq 0`$)</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq k}\bigcup_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{l}\leq k}E_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(28)</span> |

<span id="A1.SS3.p4.7.1" class="ltx_text">This finishes the proof as
desired. ∎</span>

</div>

<div id="A1.SS5" class="section ltx_subsection">

### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_subsection">A.5 </span>Why scalarization fails in the presence of gradient disagreement

<div id="A1.SS5.p1" class="ltx_para">

We explain why linear scalarization is incapable of exploring an
intersection point with gradient disagreement.

</div>

<div id="A1.SS5.p2" class="ltx_para">

We first recall the geometric interpretation (see Figure 4.9
in \[<a href="#bib.bib2" class="ltx_ref">Boyd et al., 2004</a>\]) of
linear scalarization: a point $`P`$ lying at the boundary of the
feasible region can be achieved by scalarization, if and only if there
exists a hyperplane at $`P`$ and the feasible region lies above the
hyperplane. The normal vector of the hyperplane is proportional to the
scalarization coefficients. By definition, if a hyperplane at $`P`$ lies
below the feasible region, its normal vector must be a subgradient of
the surface. When the boundary of the feasible region is differentiable
and the subdifferential set is non-empty, the normal vector must be the
gradient of the surface, and the hyperplane becomes the tangent plane at
$`P`$.

</div>

<div id="A1.SS5.p3" class="ltx_para">

Now suppose $`P`$ lies at the intersection of two differentiable
surfaces $`S_{1}`$ and $`S_{2}`$, and that $`P`$ can be achieved by
scalarization. Applying the above argument to $`S_{1}`$ and $`P`$, we
know that the scalarization coefficients are proportional to the
gradient w.r.t. $`S_{1}`$. Similarly, applying the above argument to
$`S_{2}`$ and $`P`$ yields that the scalarization coefficients are
proportional to the gradient w.r.t. $`S_{2}`$. This will result in a
contradiction if the two gradients w.r.t. $`S_{1}`$ and $`S_{2}`$ at
$`P`$ lie in different directions, a phenomenon we refer to as
<span id="A1.SS5.p3.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">gradient
disagreement</span>. In this case, scalarization cannot reach $`P`$.

</div>

class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Lemma</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.4</span></a>

<div id="A2.SS1.p1" class="ltx_para">

We begin by reviewing some basics of convex analysis. We refer the
readers to <a href="#bib.bib34" class="ltx_ref">Rockafellar [1997]</a>
for more background knowledge.

</div>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem1" class="ltx_theorem ltx_theorem_definition">

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="A2.Thmtheorem1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Definition B.1</span></span><span id="A2.Thmtheorem1.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold"> </span>(Convex hull)<span id="A2.Thmtheorem1.4" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem1.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.Thmtheorem1.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">Let
$`X=\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}`$ be a collection of points in
$`\mathbb{R}^{n}`$. The <span id="A2.Thmtheorem1.p1.1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">convex hull</span> of $`X`$, denoted as
$`\operatorname{conv}(X)`$, is the set of all convex combinations of
points in $`X`$, i.e.,</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\operatorname{conv}(X):=\left\{\sum_{i\in[m]}\alpha_{i}x_{i}\mid\alpha_{i}\geq 0,\sum_{i\in[m]}\alpha_{i}=1\right\}.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(31)</span> |

</div>

</div>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem2" class="ltx_theorem ltx_theorem_definition">

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="A2.Thmtheorem2.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Definition B.2</span></span><span id="A2.Thmtheorem2.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold"> </span>(Cone and convex cone)<span id="A2.Thmtheorem2.4" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem2.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.Thmtheorem2.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">A set
$`K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}`$ is called a
<span id="A2.Thmtheorem2.p1.1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">cone</span> if $`x\in K`$ implies
$`\alpha x\in K`$ for all $`\alpha>0`$. A
<span id="A2.Thmtheorem2.p1.1.1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">convex
cone</span> is a cone that is closed under addition. The convex cone
generated by $`X=\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}`$, denoted as
$`\operatorname{cone}(X)`$, is given by</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\operatorname{cone}(X):=\operatorname{conv}(\operatorname{ray}(X)),
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(32)</span> |

<span id="A2.Thmtheorem2.p1.2.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">where
$`\operatorname{ray}(X)=\{\lambda x_{i}\mid\lambda\geq 0,i\in[m]\}`$.</span>

</div>

</div>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem3" class="ltx_theorem ltx_theorem_definition">

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="A2.Thmtheorem3.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Definition B.3</span></span><span id="A2.Thmtheorem3.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold"> </span>(Dual cone)<span id="A2.Thmtheorem3.4" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem3.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.Thmtheorem3.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">The
<span id="A2.Thmtheorem3.p1.1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">dual
cone</span> of $`C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}`$, denoted as $`C^{*}`$, is
given by</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
C^{*}:=\{y\mid\langle y,x\rangle\geq 0,\ \forall x\in C\}
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(33)</span> |

</div>

</div>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem4" class="ltx_theorem ltx_theorem_definition">

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="A2.Thmtheorem4.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Definition B.4</span></span><span id="A2.Thmtheorem4.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold"> </span>(Projection onto a convex set)<span id="A2.Thmtheorem4.4" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem4.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.Thmtheorem4.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">Let
$`C`$ be a closed convex set in $`\mathbb{R}^{n}`$. The
<span id="A2.Thmtheorem4.p1.1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">orthogonal projection</span> of
$`y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}`$ onto the set $`C`$, denoted as $`P_{C}y`$, is the
solution to the optimization problem</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
P_{C}y:=\inf_{x\in C}\{\|x-y\|\}.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(34)</span> |

</div>

</div>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem5" class="ltx_theorem ltx_theorem_theorem">

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="A2.Thmtheorem5.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Theorem B.5</span></span><span id="A2.Thmtheorem5.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold"> </span>(Bourbaki-Cheney-Goldstein inequality \[<a href="#bib.bib6" class="ltx_ref">Ciarlet, 2013</a>\])<span id="A2.Thmtheorem5.4" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem5.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.Thmtheorem5.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">Let
$`C`$ be a closed convex set in $`\mathbb{R}^{n}`$. For any
$`x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}`$, we have</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\langle x-P_{C}x,y-P_{C}x\rangle\leq 0,\quad\forall y\in C.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(35)</span> |

</div>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS1.p2" class="ltx_para">

Theorem <a href="#A2.Thmtheorem5" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem B.5 (Bourbaki-Cheney-Goldstein inequality [ , ]). ‣ B.1 Proof of ‣ Appendix B Omitted details from ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Theorem</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">B.5</span></a> is also known as the
variational characterization of convex projection. We will use it to
prove the following lemma, which serves as a crucial ingredient in the
proof of <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem4" class="ltx_ref"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Lemma</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.4</span></a>.

</div>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem6" class="ltx_theorem ltx_theorem_lemma">

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="A2.Thmtheorem6.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Lemma B.6</span></span><span id="A2.Thmtheorem6.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem6.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.Thmtheorem6.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">Let
$`C`$ be a closed convex set in $`\mathbb{R}^{n}`$. For any
$`z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}`$, we have</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\langle z^{\prime},x\rangle\geq\langle z,x\rangle,\quad\forall x\in C,
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(36)</span> |

<span id="A2.Thmtheorem6.p1.2.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">where
$`z^{\prime}=2P_{C}z-z`$ is the reflection of $`z`$ w.r.t. $`C`$.</span>

</div>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS1.2" class="ltx_proof">

###### Proof.

<div id="A2.SS1.p3" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.SS1.p3.1.1" class="ltx_text">Plugging in
$`z^{\prime}=2P_{C}z-z`$, it suffices to show</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\langle P_{C}z-z,x\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall x\in C.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(37)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS1.p3.2.1" class="ltx_text">This is true because</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\langle P_{C}z-z,x-P_{C}z\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall x\in C
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(38)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS1.p3.3.1"
class="ltx_text">by <a href="#A2.Thmtheorem5" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem B.5 (Bourbaki-Cheney-Goldstein inequality [ , ]). ‣ B.1 Proof of ‣ Appendix B Omitted details from ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Theorem</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">B.5</span></a>, and that</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\langle z-P_{C}z,P_{C}z\rangle=0.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(39)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS1.p3.4.1" class="ltx_text">∎</span>

</div>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS1.p4" class="ltx_para">

We are now ready to prove <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem4" class="ltx_ref"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Lemma</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.4</span></a>.

</div>

<div id="A2.SS1.3" class="ltx_proof">

###### Proof of <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem4" class="ltx_ref"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Lemma</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.4</span></a>

<div id="A2.SS1.p5" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.SS1.p5.1.1" class="ltx_text">For the
</span><span id="A2.SS1.p5.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">if</span><span id="A2.SS1.p5.1.3"
class="ltx_text"> part, we assume w.l.o.g. that
$`G=\hat{Y}^{\top}\hat{Y}`$ is doubly non-negative. This implies that
the angle between each pair of optimal predictors is non-obtuse. Denote
$`\mathcal{A}=\operatorname{cone}(\{\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i\in[k]})`$ and
$`\mathcal{A}^{*}`$ as its dual cone. We have
$`\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{A}^{*}`$.</span>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS1.p6" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.SS1.p6.1.1" class="ltx_text">Our goal is to show that, for
every $`\|s\|=1`$, we can always find $`s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}`$,
such that 1) $`\|s^{\prime}\|=1`$; 2)
$`\langle s^{\prime},\hat{y}_{i}\rangle\geq|\langle s,\hat{y}_{i}\rangle|,\ \forall i\in[k]`$.
This implies that when restricting our discussion to the Pareto front,
we can safely ignore the absolute value which appears in the proof
of <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem1" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem 3.1. ‣ 3.1 The multi-surface structure of the feasible region ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Theorem</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.1</span></a>. As a consequence, the
Pareto front belongs to the surface determined by $`Q=G^{-1}`$.</span>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS1.p7" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.SS1.p7.1.1" class="ltx_text">Consider
$`s^{\prime}=2P_{\mathcal{A}^{*}}s-s`$. It is straightforward to see
that $`\|s^{\prime}\|=1`$ since it is the reflection of $`s`$ w.r.t.
$`\mathcal{A}^{*}`$. To show 2), we will prove:</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\langle s^{\prime},x\rangle\geq|\langle s,x\rangle|,\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{A}.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(40)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS1.p7.2.1" class="ltx_text">We break the discussion into
two cases.</span>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS1.p8" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.SS1.p8.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Case 1:
$`\langle s,x\rangle\geq 0`$.<span id="A2.SS1.p8.1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_medium"> Since
$`x\in\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{A}^{*}`$, we have</span></span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\langle s^{\prime},x\rangle\geq\langle s,x\rangle=|\langle s,x\rangle|
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(41)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS1.p8.2.1"
class="ltx_text">by <a href="#A2.Thmtheorem6" class="ltx_ref"
title="Lemma B.6. ‣ B.1 Proof of ‣ Appendix B Omitted details from ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Lemma</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">B.6</span></a>.</span>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS1.p9" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.SS1.p9.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Case 2:
$`\langle s,x\rangle<0`$. <span id="A2.SS1.p9.1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_medium"> Consider $`u=-s`$ and denote
$`\mathcal{C}:=\{a\mid\langle a,P_{\mathcal{A}^{*}}s\rangle\geq 0\}`$.
It is straightforward to see that
$`\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{C}`$.</span></span>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS1.p10" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.SS1.p10.1.1" class="ltx_text">We will show
$`P_{\mathcal{C}}u=P_{\mathcal{A}^{*}}s-s`$. In fact, for any
$`z\in\mathcal{C}`$, we have</span>

``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(43)</span> |

|  | 
``` math
g(a,b,c)=q_{11}a+q_{22}b+q_{33}c+2q_{12}\sqrt{ab}+2q_{13}\sqrt{ac}-2q_{23}\sqrt{bc}.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(53)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS4.p9.2.1" class="ltx_text">We have</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle g(a,b,c)`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq g(q_{23}^{2}/r,b,c)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq g(q_{23}^{2}/r,q_{13}^{2}/r,c)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq g(q_{23}^{2}/r,q_{13}^{2}/r,q_{12}^{2}/r)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=1,`$ |  |

<span id="A2.SS4.p9.3.1" class="ltx_text">where the equalities hold iff
$`I=J`$. This finishes the proof as desired. ∎</span>

</div>

### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_subsection">B.5 </span>Proof of <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem10" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem 3.10 (Sufficiency of C1 (resp. C2)). ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Theorem</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.10</span></a>

<div id="A2.SS5.p1" class="ltx_para">

We first present several useful lemmas regarding non-negative matrices.

</div>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem7" class="ltx_theorem ltx_theorem_lemma">

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="A2.Thmtheorem7.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Lemma B.7</span></span><span id="A2.Thmtheorem7.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem7.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.Thmtheorem7.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">Let
$`A`$ be a non-negative, irreducible and real symmetric matrix, then
there only exists one eigenvector $`v`$ of $`A`$ (up to scaling), such
that $`v>0`$.</span>

</div>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS5.2" class="ltx_proof">

###### Proof.

<div id="A2.SS5.p2" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.SS5.p2.1.1" class="ltx_text">Since $`A`$ is non-negative
and irreducible, by Perron-Frobenius
theorem \[<a href="#bib.bib38" class="ltx_ref">Shaked-Monderer and Berman,
2021</a>\], the eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue
$`\rho(A)`$ (a.k.a. </span><span id="A2.SS5.p2.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">Perron
root</span><span id="A2.SS5.p2.1.3" class="ltx_text">) can be made
positive, and is unique up to scaling. We denote it as $`v`$.</span>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS5.p3" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.SS5.p3.1.1" class="ltx_text">We will show the eigenvectors
associated with other eigenvalues cannot be positive. In fact, assume
$`u>0`$ is an eigenvector associated with $`\lambda<\rho(A)`$. Since
$`A`$ is symmetric, we have</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\rho(A)v^{\top}u=v^{\top}A^{\top}u=v^{\top}Au=\lambda v^{\top}u.`$ |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(54)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS5.p3.2.1" class="ltx_text">This is a contradiction since
$`v^{\top}u>0`$ and $`\rho(A)>\lambda`$. ∎</span>

</div>

</div>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem8" class="ltx_theorem ltx_theorem_lemma">

###### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_theorem"><span id="A2.Thmtheorem8.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Lemma B.8</span></span><span id="A2.Thmtheorem8.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">.</span>

<div id="A2.Thmtheorem8.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.Thmtheorem8.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">Let
$`A`$ be a non-negative and real symmetric matrix. Suppose $`v`$ is an
eigenvector of $`A`$ such that $`v>0`$, then $`v`$ corresponds to the
largest eigenvalue of $`A`$.</span>

</div>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS5.3" class="ltx_proof">

###### Proof.

<div id="A2.SS5.p4" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.SS5.p4.1.1" class="ltx_text">Consider the normal
form \[<a href="#bib.bib41" class="ltx_ref">Varga, 1999</a>\] of
$`A`$:</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle PAP^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}B_{11}&B_{12}&\cdots&B_{1h}\\
0&B_{22}&\cdots&B_{2h}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&B_{hh}\end{bmatrix}\triangleq B,`$ |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(55)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS5.p4.2.1" class="ltx_text">where $`P`$ is a permutation
matrix and $`B_{ii}`$ ($`i\in[h]`$) are irreducible. It is
straightforward to see that $`B`$ is non-negative. Furthermore, since a
permutation matrix is an orthogonal matrix, $`B`$ is also symmetric
(implying that $`B_{ij}=0`$ for $`i\neq j`$) and has the same spectrum
as $`A`$.</span>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS5.p5" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.SS5.p5.1.1" class="ltx_text">Now suppose $`Av=\lambda v`$
for some $`v>0`$. Since $`B=PAP^{\top}`$, we have $`B\psi=\lambda\psi`$
with $`\psi=Pv>0`$. Denote</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{n}\end{pmatrix},`$ |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(56)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS5.p5.2.1" class="ltx_text">where each $`\psi_{i}`$ has
the same dimension as $`B_{ii}`$. Now we have</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle B_{ii}\psi_{i}=\lambda\psi_{i},\quad\forall i\in[h].`$ |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(57)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS5.p5.3.1" class="ltx_text">Since $`B_{ii}`$ is
non-negative, irreducible and real symmetric,
by <a href="#A2.Thmtheorem7" class="ltx_ref"
title="Lemma B.7. ‣ B.5 Proof of ‣ Appendix B Omitted details from ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Lemma</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">B.7</span></a>, $`\psi_{i}`$ corresponds to
the largest eigenvalue of $`B_{ii}`$. Note $`B`$ is a block diagonal
matrix, so the spectrum of $`B`$ is essentially the union of the
spectrum of $`B_{ii}`$. As a consequence,</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{max}}(A)=\lambda_{\text{max}}(B)=\max_{i\in[h]}\lambda_{\text{max}}(B_{ii})=\lambda.`$ |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(58)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS5.p5.4.1" class="ltx_text">∎</span>

</div>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS5.4" class="ltx_proof">

###### Proof of <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem10" class="ltx_ref"
title="Theorem 3.10 (Sufficiency of C1 (resp. C2)). ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Theorem</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.10</span></a>

<div id="A2.SS5.p6" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.SS5.p6.1.1" class="ltx_text">We prove the sufficiency of
<a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a>
and <a href="#c2" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.5. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C2</a>
separately.</span>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS5.p7" class="ltx_para">

<a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter ltx_font_bold"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a><span id="A2.SS5.p7.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold"> is sufficient.<span id="A2.SS5.p7.1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_medium">  We have demonstrated
in <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem4" class="ltx_ref"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Lemma</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.4</span></a> that the Pareto front
belongs to a single surface $`E`$, whose gram matrix is doubly
non-negative. Let $`p=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{k})^{\top}`$ be a PO of $`E`$,
and denote $`t=\sqrt{p}`$. We will show that the tangent plane at this
point lies above the feasible region, implying that it can be achieved
by scalarization.</span></span>

</div>

<div id="A2.SS5.p8" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A2.SS5.p8.1.1" class="ltx_text">Concretely, the tangent plane
is given by</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\sum_{i\in[k]}\frac{\sum_{j\in[k]}q_{ij}\sqrt{a_{j}}}{\sqrt{a_{i}}}v_{i}=1.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(59)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS5.p8.2.1" class="ltx_text">To show it lies above the
feasible region, it suffices to prove that it lies above every surface
in $`\mathcal{E}`$, i.e.,</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\sum_{i\in[k]}\frac{\sum_{j\in[k]}q_{ij}\sqrt{a_{j}}}{\sqrt{a_{i}}}v_{i}\leq\sum_{i\in[k]}q_{ii}^{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}v_{i}+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}q_{ij}^{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}\sqrt{v_{i}v_{j}},
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(60)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS5.p8.3.1" class="ltx_text">where
$`q_{ij}^{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}`$ represents the $`(i,j)`$-th entry of
$`Q_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}`$. Note <a href="#A2.E60" class="ltx_ref"
title="In Proof of ‣ B.5 Proof of ‣ Appendix B Omitted details from ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Equation</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">60</span></a> can be treated as a quadratic
form w.r.t. $`\sqrt{v}`$, so it suffices to prove the corresponding
matrix is PSD. Writing compactly, it suffices to show</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
Q_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}-\diag\{Qt\oslash t\}=D_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}(Q-\diag\{Qt\oslash t\})D_{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(61)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS5.p8.4.1" class="ltx_text">is PSD. As a consequence, we
only need to show the positive semi-definiteness of the following
matrix</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
T:=Q-\diag\{Qt\oslash t\}.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(62)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS5.p8.5.1" class="ltx_text">Since $`p`$ is a PO of $`E`$,
we have $`Qt\geq 0`$. Let $`w=Qt`$, we have</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
T=G^{-1}-\diag\{w\oslash Gw\}.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(63)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS5.p8.6.1" class="ltx_text">Assume w.l.o.g. that $`w>0`$
(otherwise we simply remove the corresponding rows and columns in
$`G^{-1}`$). Denote</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
R=\sqrt{\diag\{w\oslash Gw\}}\quad\text{and}\quad\phi=\sqrt{w\odot Gw}>0.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(64)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS5.p8.7.1" class="ltx_text">We have</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle RGR\phi`$ | $`\displaystyle=RG\sqrt{\diag\{w\oslash Gw\}}\sqrt{w\odot Gw}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=RGw`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sqrt{\diag\{w\oslash Gw\}}Gw`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sqrt{w\odot Gw}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\phi.`$ |  |

<span id="A2.SS5.p8.8.1"
class="ltx_text">By <a href="#A2.Thmtheorem8" class="ltx_ref"
title="Lemma B.8. ‣ B.5 Proof of ‣ Appendix B Omitted details from ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Lemma</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">B.8</span></a>, we have $`\rho(RGR)=1`$.
Since $`RGR`$ is positive definite, we have</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
I\succeq RGR\succ 0.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(65)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS5.p8.9.1" class="ltx_text">This further implies that
$`R^{-2}\succeq G`$, and</span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
T=G^{-1}-R^{2}\succeq 0.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(66)</span> |

</div>

<div id="A2.SS5.p9" class="ltx_para">

<a href="#c2" class="ltx_ref ltx_font_typewriter ltx_font_bold"
title="Lemma 3.5. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C2</a><span id="A2.SS5.p9.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold"> is sufficient.<span id="A2.SS5.p9.1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_medium">  We have demonstrated
in <a href="#S3.Thmtheorem5" class="ltx_ref"
title="Lemma 3.5. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Lemma</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3.5</span></a> that the Pareto front
belongs to a single surface $`I_{\varnothing}`$. Here we will further
show $`I_{\varnothing}`$ is concave, and apply the supporting hyperplane
theorem \[<a href="#bib.bib2" class="ltx_ref">Boyd et al., 2004</a>,
Section 2.5.2\] to conclude the proof. In fact, $`E_{\varnothing}`$ is
parametrized by</span></span>

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | 
``` math
\sum_{i\in[k]}q_{ii}v_{i}+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}q_{ij}\sqrt{v_{i}v_{j}}=1.
``` |  | <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_equation ltx_align_right">(67)</span> |

<span id="A2.SS5.p9.2.1" class="ltx_text">Since the first term is a
summation of linear functions, $`f(x,y)=\sqrt{xy}`$ is a concave
function and that $`q_{ij}\geq 0`$, we conclude that $`E_{\varnothing}`$
is convex, and $`I_{\varnothing}`$ is concave. This finishes the proof
as desired. ∎</span>

</div>

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_appendix">Appendix C </span>A polynomial algorithm for checking <span id="A3.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_typewriter">C1</span>

<div id="A3.p1" class="ltx_para">

We explain how <a href="#alg1" class="ltx_ref"
title="In Appendix C A polynomial algorithm for checking C1 ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Algorithm</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">1</span></a> works.

</div>

<div id="A3.p2" class="ltx_para">

Essentially, the goal is to find a set of flipping signs
$`\{s_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}\in\{+,-\}`$ that ensure positive correlation
between all pairs in $`\{s_{i}\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}`$ (except for
those pairs that are not correlated). We state an observation which is
critical for our algorithm—given a pair $`\hat{y}_{i}`$ and
$`\hat{y}_{j}`$, whether their signs are the same or opposite can be
determined by whether $`\hat{y}_{i}`$ and $`\hat{y}_{j}`$ is positively
or negatively correlated. Thus, given a determined $`s_{i}`$, the signs
of its subsequent predictors $`s_{j},\ j>i`$ can be uniquely determined
by evaluating $`\langle\hat{y}_{i},\hat{y}_{j}\rangle`$. This allows us
to determine the signs for the predictors $`s_{i}`$ in ascending order,
and check for potential conflicts in the mean time.

</div>

<div id="A3.p3" class="ltx_para">

We state one loop invariant in our algorithm—when entering iteration
$`i`$ in the outer loop, $`s_{i}`$ has been determined by its
<span id="A3.p3.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">preceding</span>
predictors and no conflict has occurred, or it has remained undetermined
(i.e., <span id="A3.p3.1.2"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_typewriter">None</span>) all the way because it
is not correlated with any preceding predictors. At this point, what is
left to be done is to examine the <span id="A3.p3.1.3"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">subsequent</span> predictors. If
$`s_{i}`$ is undetermined (lines 2-7), we attempt to use its subsequent
predictors to determine it (line 5), and break out of the loop once it
gets determined. Now that we make sure that $`s_{i}`$ is determined
after line 9, we can proceed to check the remaining subsequent
predictors, to either determine their signs (lines 12-13) or check for
potential conflicts (lines 14-15).

</div>

<div id="A3.p4" class="ltx_para">

We comment that caution needs to be taken for the pairs that are not
correlated (i.e., $`\langle\hat{y}_{i},\hat{y}_{j}\rangle=0`$). In this
case, one cannot determine the sign of $`s_{j}`$ by the relationship
between these two predictors; basically, the sign has no influence on
their correlation. Thus, the predictor $`\hat{y}_{j}`$ retains the
flexibility to be determined by other predictors other than
$`\hat{y}_{i}`$. Additionally, it is possible that at the end of line 9
$`s_{j}`$ is still undetermined; this only occurs when $`\hat{y}_{i}`$
is not correlated with any other optimal predictor. In this case, $`t`$
will take the value of $`k+1`$ and the following loop (lines 10-15) will
not be executed.

</div>

<div id="A4" class="section ltx_appendix">

## <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_appendix">Appendix D </span>Additional experimental details and results

<div id="A4.SS1" class="section ltx_subsection">

### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_subsection">D.1 </span>Additional experimental details

<div id="A4.SS1.p1" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A4.SS1.p1.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Dataset.</span> The SARCOS
dataset \[<a href="#bib.bib42" class="ltx_ref">Vijayakumar and Schaal, 2000</a>\]
consists of the configurations (position, velocity, acceleration) of the
robotic arms, and the problem is to predict the torque of the respective
arm given its triplet configuration. Since our study concentrates on the
training procedure and does not concern generalization, we conduct the
experiments on the training set alone, where the size of the training
set of the SARCOS
dataset \[<a href="#bib.bib42" class="ltx_ref">Vijayakumar and Schaal, 2000</a>\]
is $`40,000`$. Regression tasks normally benefit from standardization as
a pro-processing
step \[<a href="#bib.bib14" class="ltx_ref">Hastie et al., 2009</a>\],
so we standardize the dataset to zero mean and unit variance.

</div>

<div id="A4.SS1.p2" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A4.SS1.p2.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Tasks.</span> Following our theoretical
analysis in <a href="#S3" class="ltx_ref"
title="3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3</span></a>, we study the scenario where
$`q=1`$. In this case, it is straightforward to see that the minimal
$`k`$ that may lead to a violation of
<a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_markedasmath ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a>
is $`3`$. To show the violation of
<a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_markedasmath ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a>
leading to the failure of scalarization exploring the Pareto Front, we
constrain our study to $`k\geq 3`$; furthermore, for ease of
visualization, we focus on $`k=3`$. In our experiments, we take the arms
$`3`$, $`4`$, and $`5`$ for which do not satisfy
<a href="#c1" class="ltx_ref ltx_markedasmath ltx_font_typewriter"
title="Lemma 3.4. ‣ 3.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for full exploration ‣ 3 Linear scalarization through the lens of exploring the Pareto front ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective">C1</a>.

</div>

<div id="A4.SS1.p3" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A4.SS1.p3.1.1"
class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Network.</span> We use a two-layer linear
network for the reason explained in <a href="#S2" class="ltx_ref"
title="2 Preliminaries ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">2</span></a>. We merge the bias term $`b`$
into the weight matrix $`W`$ by adding an additional all-one column to
the input $`X`$. The input dimension of the network is $`22`$ (seven
(position, velocity, acceleration) triplets plus the dummy feature
induced by $`b`$), the hidden size is $`q=1`$, and the output size is
$`1`$ (i.e., the predicted torque). The same network architecture is
used for both experiments in scalarization and that in SMTOs.

</div>

<div id="A4.SS1.p4" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A4.SS1.p4.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Implementation
of linear scalarization.</span>  For linear scalarization, we uniformly
sample $`100,000`$ sets of convex coefficients from a three-dimensional
simplex (i.e., a tetrahedron). Concretely, we first sample
$`m_{1},m_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}U(0,1)`$, and then
craft
$`\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})=(\min(m_{1},m_{2}),\max(m_{1},m_{2})-\min(m_{1},m_{2}),1-\max(m_{1},m_{2})+\min(m_{1},m_{2}))`$
as the weights. For a given set of convex coefficients, we calculate the
optimal value directly based on the analysis
in <a href="#S2" class="ltx_ref"
title="2 Preliminaries ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">2</span></a> instead of performing actual
training. More specifically, the optimum of the scalarization objective
is given by
$`\hat{Y}_{q,\Lambda}\Lambda\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{q}\sigma_{i}u_{i}v_{i}^{\top}`$,
from which we can compute the corresponding MSE for each task.

</div>

<div id="A4.SS1.p5" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A4.SS1.p5.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">SMTOs.</span> We
consider two state-of-the-art SMTOs,
MGDA \[<a href="#bib.bib9" class="ltx_ref">Désidéri, 2012</a>\] and
MGDA-UB \[<a href="#bib.bib37" class="ltx_ref">Sener and Koltun, 2018</a>\].
MGDA is an optimization algorithm specifically devised for handling
multiple objectives concurrently. It utilizes a composite gradient
direction to facilitate simultaneous progress across all objectives.
MGDA-UB is an efficient variant of MGDA that focuses on maximizing the
minimum improvement among all objectives. It is a gradient-based
multi-objective optimization algorithm aimed at achieving balanced
optimization outcomes across all objectives.

</div>

<div id="A4.SS1.p6" class="ltx_para">

<span id="A4.SS1.p6.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Implementation
of SMTOs.</span>  Our code is based on the released
implementation<span id="footnote3"
class="ltx_note ltx_role_footnote"><sup>3</sup><span class="ltx_note_outer"><span class="ltx_note_content"><sup>3</sup>
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_note">3</span>
<a href="https://github.com/isl-org/MultiObjectiveOptimization"
class="ltx_ref ltx_url ltx_font_typewriter">https://github.com/isl-org/MultiObjectiveOptimization</a></span></span></span>
of MGDA-UB, which also includes the code for MGDA. We apply their code
on the SARCOS dataset. For both methods, we use vanilla gradient descent
with a learning rate of $`0.5`$ for $`100`$ epochs, following the
default choice in the released implementation. We comment that early
stopping can also be adopted, i.e., terminate once the minimum norm of
the convex hull of gradients is smaller than a threshold, for which we
set to be $`10^{-3}`$.

</div>

</div>

### <span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_subsection">D.3 </span>Additional experiments on randomization

<div id="A4.SS3.p1" class="ltx_para">

To verify the effectiveness of randomization as discussed
in <a href="#S4.SS2" class="ltx_ref"
title="4.2 A remedy solution ‣ 4 Discussion ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Section</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">4.2</span></a>, we plot the region
achievable by randomized linear scalarization. Specifically, according
to <a href="#S4.Ex2" class="ltx_ref"
title="In 4.2 A remedy solution ‣ 4 Discussion ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">13</span></a>, the region can be expressed
as the collection of the convex combination of two points from the
feasible region. To this end, we randomly sample 100,000 weight pairs
(the same number as in <a href="#S5.F3" class="ltx_ref"
title="In 5 Experiments ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Figure</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3</span></a>). For each weight pair
$`(w_{1},w_{2})`$, we uniformly draw $`t\sim U(0,1)`$ and get two
corresponding optimal networks $`f_{1}`$ and $`f_{2}`$ by SVD. For each
sample, with probability $`t`$, the model uses $`f_{1}`$, otherwise
$`f_{2}`$, to calculate the MSE. The final result is demonstrated
in <a href="#A4.F5" class="ltx_ref"
title="In D.3 Additional experiments on randomization ‣ Appendix D Additional experimental details and results ‣ Revisiting Scalarization in Multi-Task Learning: A Theoretical Perspective"><span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Figure</span> <span
class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">5</span></a>. It is straightforward to see
that randomization allows scalarization to trace out the PF since there
is no hole within the blue region, thus validating our theoretical
analysis. We additionally comment that randomization convexifies the
feasible region, as such, the solutions found by MGDA and MGDA-UB are
dominated.

</div>
